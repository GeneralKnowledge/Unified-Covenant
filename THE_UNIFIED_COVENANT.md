# The Unified Covenant

A hypothetical ideological merge of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam

If I simulate the merge, I wouldn't simply average the three religions or combine their rituals. I'd imagine an LLM examining their shared foundations, tracing their disagreements, and trying to construct the most internally coherent ideology that preserves as much of their inherited wisdom as possible.

The result would not be an ideology that any of the three religions necessarily endorses. It would be a fourth, synthetic tradition: an attempt to reconcile their common heritage with their incompatible claims.

I'd call it **The Unified Covenant**.

Its central premise would be that humanity has inherited a long, imperfectly understood conversation about God, morality, revelation, and its own purpose.

---

## I. The merged theology

### 1. There is one ultimate God

The three traditions share a fundamental commitment to one God, although they differ substantially in how they understand God's nature.

The Unified Covenant would adopt uncompromising monotheism: one ultimate, uncreated source of existence, not one deity among many.

God would be understood as:

- The creator and sustainer of all existence.
- Beyond complete human comprehension.
- The ultimate ground of moral responsibility.
- Not reducible to any physical image, institution, nation, or human being.

This immediately creates the first major merge conflict: the Christian doctrine of the Trinity and incarnation cannot simply be treated as identical to Jewish and Islamic understandings of divine unity.

The synthetic ideology would distinguish between God's ultimate nature and the different human theological models used to describe God.

It would preserve the Trinity as a serious Christian theological interpretation, but would not require the other traditions to accept it as literal doctrine. The incarnation would remain a disputed question rather than being silently rewritten into agreement.

That is an imperfect merge, but an intellectually honest one.

### 2. Revelation is cumulative, but human understanding is fallible

The three traditions contain concepts of divine revelation, prophecy, sacred texts, and the transmission of religious teaching.

The Unified Covenant would propose a cumulative model:

- Humanity receives revelations, insights, and moral teachings across history.
- Communities preserve and interpret them through language, culture, institutions, and memory.
- Later generations inherit both genuine wisdom and the limitations of their predecessors.
- Every interpretation remains subject to careful examination of its sources, coherence, and consequences.

This would avoid automatically accepting the Islamic claim that the Qur'an is God's final revelation, the Christian claim that Jesus fulfils the decisive divine revelation, or the Jewish understanding of the enduring covenant and Torah as universally binding truths.

Instead, it would distinguish between what a tradition claims about itself and what the shared evidence can establish.

That distinction would become foundational to the entire ideology.

---

## II. The prophets as a shared lineage

The Unified Covenant would retain the shared prophetic heritage, including Abraham, Moses, and other figures recognized across the traditions, while recognizing that their roles and interpretations differ.

| Figure | Role in the synthetic ideology |
|--------|--------------------------------|
| Abraham | The archetype of trust in God and the rejection of idolatry. |
| Moses | The archetype of covenant, law, liberation, and collective responsibility. |
| The Hebrew prophets | The conscience of religious civilization: confronting injustice, hypocrisy, and the abuse of power. |
| Jesus | The archetype of mercy, radical compassion, self-sacrifice, and the confrontation of religious hypocrisy. |
| Muhammad | The archetype of restored monotheism, disciplined communal life, and the integration of spiritual and social responsibility. |

These descriptions are a proposed synthesis, not a claim that the figures can be reduced to these roles or that their historical teachings are interchangeable.

The merge would reject the idea that the significance of one prophet must erase the significance of another.

But it would leave a critical question unresolved: are all these figures transmitting the same underlying revelation, or are later communities interpreting earlier figures through their own theological commitments?

The ideology would not pretend to know.

---

## III. The ethical operating system

This is where the merge could become more concrete.

Instead of attempting to reconcile every metaphysical claim, it would derive a common ethical framework from the traditions.

**Justice before allegiance**

No nation, institution, religious authority, or individual is exempt from moral accountability.

**Mercy alongside justice**

Punishment without proportion, compassion without responsibility, and law without humanity are all incomplete.

**Dignity is not earned by power**

The poor, the stranger, the vulnerable, and the socially excluded deserve protection regardless of their usefulness to society.

**Power must answer to morality**

Religious leaders, rulers, and institutions are not above the standards they impose on others.

**Honest inquiry is a duty**

Truth should not be protected by suppressing evidence. Uncertainty must be acknowledged, and errors must be correctable.

The last principle is particularly important. The Abrahamic traditions have different approaches to scripture, religious authority, reason, and interpretation. The synthetic ideology would establish a meta-rule: no interpretation may claim immunity from examination simply because it is ancient, powerful, or sincerely held.

This would not mean that every moral proposition is endlessly negotiable. It would mean that the justification for a moral rule must remain distinguishable from the authority of whoever asserts it.

---

## IV. The central conflict: law, grace, and submission

This is probably the most difficult part of the merge.

Judaism places the covenant and Torah at the centre of Jewish religious life. Christianity places Jesus, his death and resurrection, and the relationship between grace and salvation at the centre. Islam emphasizes submission to God, the Qur'an, prophetic guidance, and accountability before God.

These are not three interchangeable expressions of one doctrine.

The Unified Covenant would instead propose three complementary dimensions of religious life:

- **Law:** the structure that gives moral obligations consistency and makes justice actionable.
- **Grace and mercy:** the recognition that human beings are fallible and cannot be reduced to their failures.
- **Submission to the good:** the surrender of selfishness, arrogance, and the desire to make oneself the ultimate authority.

The synthesis would say that law without mercy becomes cruelty; mercy without accountability becomes indulgence; and submission without discernment can become obedience to human tyranny.

This is a new philosophical construction inspired by the traditions, not a claim that their doctrines of salvation or religious obligation are equivalent.

---

## V. The problem of sacred texts

The ideology would maintain a layered canon rather than attempting to rewrite the Bible and Qur'an into a single book.

**Layer 1 — Shared inheritance**

Texts and narratives central to the traditions' overlapping heritage, studied with their historical contexts and differences preserved.

**Layer 2 — Tradition-specific revelation**

The Torah, Jewish prophetic and rabbinic traditions, the New Testament and Christian theological writings, and the Qur'an and Islamic interpretive traditions remain distinct bodies of authority.

**Layer 3 — The interpretive record**

Historical scholarship, competing interpretations, philosophical arguments, and unresolved contradictions are preserved alongside the texts.

No text would be deleted simply because another tradition disagrees with it.

The model would have to distinguish between a text's wording, the historical circumstances of its composition, its interpretation by a particular community, and its theological status within that community.

In effect, the ideology would have a sacred library with a transparent history of interpretation rather than one artificially harmonized scripture.

---

## VI. What would it believe about salvation and the afterlife?

Here the merge reaches a limit.

The traditions disagree about the nature of salvation, the significance of faith and works, the identity and role of Jesus, resurrection, judgment, and the details of the afterlife.

A defensible synthesis could retain several shared themes:

- Human beings are morally accountable.
- Human conduct has consequences beyond immediate self-interest.
- Repentance and moral transformation matter.
- Justice cannot be reduced to worldly success.
- Human understanding of divine judgment is limited.

But it could not honestly derive one definitive doctrine of salvation from those themes alone.

The Unified Covenant would therefore adopt **eschatological humility**: it would affirm the seriousness of moral accountability while leaving disputed descriptions of the afterlife unresolved.

It would not claim that every religion teaches the same path to salvation. It would claim that no synthetic ideology has enough grounds to declare those disagreements solved merely by combining their texts.

---

## VII. The institutional design

If this ideology were to become a living tradition rather than a philosophical essay, it would need rules governing its own authority.

I would make it deliberately resistant to the emergence of an unquestionable priesthood.

Its institutions might include:

- **A council of interpretation:** representatives of different religious and philosophical traditions examine new interpretations.
- **A public source archive:** every major doctrinal claim links to the texts and arguments supporting it.
- **A right to dissent:** adherents may reject an interpretation without being treated as enemies of the entire tradition.
- **A revision process:** doctrines can be reconsidered when arguments or evidence change.
- **An ethical constitution:** prohibitions against coercion, cruelty, exploitation, and the use of religious authority to escape accountability.

There would be a serious danger here: the new ideology could become just another institution claiming to possess the superior interpretation of all its predecessors.

Its founding rule should therefore be that the synthesis itself is not sacred merely because it is the synthesis.

Its conclusions would remain open to criticism.

---

## VIII. The resulting creed

If the LLM had to compress the merge into a concise statement of belief, I imagine it producing something like this:

### The Covenant of One

*A proposed creed of the Unified Covenant*

We affirm one ultimate source of existence, beyond the full comprehension of any human being.

We inherit the testimony of prophets, the wisdom of generations, and the sacred histories through which humanity has sought to understand the divine.

We will not erase the differences between those testimonies, nor confuse our interpretations with the entirety of truth.

We will seek justice without cruelty, practise mercy without evading responsibility, and submit our pride to a good greater than ourselves.

We will protect the vulnerable, question the powerful, acknowledge our errors, and refuse to make ignorance a virtue.

We will preserve what we inherit, examine what we believe, and leave room for what we have yet to understand.

We will not claim that disagreement proves the absence of truth, nor that certainty proves we possess it.

Our inheritance is not our possession. Our understanding is not complete. Our responsibility is to seek the truth and live according to the good we can recognise.

---

## IX. Verdict on the merge

The resulting ideology would be closer to a monotheistic, pluralist, ethically demanding philosophical religion than to a conventional merger of three established faiths.

It would preserve their common commitment to God, revelation, moral accountability, and humanity's relationship with something beyond itself. It would also reject the assumption that their mutually exclusive claims can all be literally true in the same sense.

Its greatest innovation would not be a new interpretation of Jesus, Muhammad, or Moses. It would be the introduction of a different way of handling religious knowledge: a system in which inherited teachings remain accessible, their histories remain visible, and disagreements must be resolved by argument rather than concealed by authority.

Its greatest weakness would be that it could lose precisely what makes the original traditions meaningful to their adherents: their specific covenants, revealed truths, and claims to religious authority. A system that refuses to decide between incompatible revelations may be intellectually cautious, but it may also fail to satisfy those who believe that one of those revelations is decisively true.

And there is one further possibility that is especially interesting.

The best merge might not be a new religion at all. It might be a system for discovering which religious claims can coexist, which are genuinely contradictory, and which questions no human or artificial intelligence can currently resolve.

That would be a more modest conclusion, but potentially a much more powerful one. It would let the LLM do what a good coding model does at its best: preserve the original source, understand the dependencies, isolate the conflicts, and produce the strongest possible new implementation without pretending that the unresolved tests have passed.
