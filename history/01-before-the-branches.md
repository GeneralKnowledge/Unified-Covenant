## Prologue: A conversation before the names

Long before anyone spoke of “Judaism,” “Christianity,” or “Islam,” the eastern Mediterranean hosted dense networks of city-states, empires, pastoral groups, and village farmers whose worlds were saturated with gods, kings, oaths, and stories. The Hebrew Bible later presents Israel’s God as the creator of heaven and earth who enters history through covenants and prophets. Islam’s Qur’an will present Abraham as a monotheist *ḥanīf* who submitted to God. Christianity will read Abraham as the father of faith fulfilled in Christ.

Those later visions look backward. Historians look **sideways and downward**: sideways to Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Anatolian, and Levantine cultures that shared motifs and institutions; downward to strata of settlement, inscription, and artifact that do not always match the literary arc of scripture.

The question this chapter must answer is narrow but foundational: **What can be responsibly said about the world that produced Israelite religion, and about the earliest recoverable shape of that religion itself?** Everything later in the project—Second Temple Judaism, rabbinic tradition, the Jesus movement, early Christianity, the rise of Islam—depends on not fantasizing a clean “root commit” where the evidence shows instead a braided river delta.

## A. The ancient Near Eastern environment

### A landscape of difference, not uniformity

The **ancient Near East** is not one culture but a mosaic: Egypt’s Nile civilization; Mesopotamia’s Sumerian, Akkadian, Assyrian, and Babylonian sequences; Anatolian Hittite power; and the **Levant**—Canaan, Phoenicia, Philistia, Ammon, Moab, Aram, and the highlands later associated with Israel and Judah. **Well established:** these regions traded, fought, translated each other’s languages, and borrowed literary forms across millennia (see general surveys such as Snell, *Religions of the Ancient Near East*; van der Toorn, *Family Religion in Babylonia, Ugarit and Israel*).

**Contested but influential:** how directly Ugarit (in modern Ras Shamra, Syria)—destroyed ca. 1180 BCE—mirrors **southern Levantine** “Canaanite” religion a century later. Ugaritic tablets remain our richest West Semitic mythological library, but Mark S. Smith cautions against “pan-Ugariticism”: Ugarit is one witness among many, not a photocopy of every local cult (**widely accepted interpretation**; Smith, *The Early History of God*, 2nd ed., preface).

Religions were **not** interchangeable. Egyptian theology tied kingship to maat and cosmic order; Mesopotamian cities housed patron deities in ziggurats; Hittite state cult integrated storm-gods and treaty oaths. Levantine religion mixed **urban temple cults**, **village shrines**, and **family rites** (offerings to ancestors and household gods). Deities had specialties: storm and fertility, sun and justice, death and the underworld.

### Kingship, law, community, and gods

In much of the Near East, **kingship was sacral**. The king could be “shepherd” or “son” of a god; victory in war demonstrated divine favor; temples received booty and taxation. Law codes—famously Hammurabi’s, but also lesser collections—presented justice as royal gift under divine witness (**well established** for Mesopotamian and Hittite parallels; see Roth, *Law Collections from Mesopotamia and Asia Minor*).

Community identity mixed **kinship**, **land**, **language**, and **patron deities**. A people might worship a national god while acknowledging that other nations had their own gods—a pattern important for understanding Israelite **monolatry** (exclusive worship of Yahweh) before full **monotheism** (denial that other gods exist), a distinction developed below.

### Shared motifs that later traditions inherit or react against

Several motifs recur across the region and reappear—transformed—in biblical literature:

| Motif | Near Eastern context | Relevance to later Abrahamic history |
|--------|----------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Divine council / assembly of gods | Ugaritic pantheon; Mesopotamian *puḫru* | Hebrew *bənê hāʾĕlōhîm*, Psalm 82, 1 Kings 22 (**contested** dating of Israelite texts) |
| Storm-god as warrior and king | Baal in Ugarit; Hadad in Aram | Yahweh language of storm, cloud, and victory (**widely accepted interpretation** of shared idiom) |
| El as high god / “father of gods” | Ugaritic *Il* | Israelite *ʾĒl*, *ʾĒl ʿElyōn*, possible convergence with Yahweh (**contested** timeline) |
| Temple as divine dwelling | Egypt, Mesopotamia, Levant | Jerusalem temple theology (**later** than this chapter’s core) |
| Flood and creation accounts | Mesopotamian *Atrahasis*, *Enuma Elish* | Genesis parallels via literary adaptation, not verbatim identity (**widely accepted interpretation**) |
| Treaty / covenant forms | Hittite vassal treaties; Neo-Assyrian loyalty oaths | Israelite *bərît* (covenant) language (**contested** direct dependence) |

**Well established:** West Semitic languages and poetic conventions link Ugaritic and Hebrew. **Well established:** the Hebrew word *ʾel* (“god” / El) appears in Semitic onomastics and texts across the region. **Not established:** that Israelite religion was “really” Canaanite religion under another name—a reductionism most specialists reject because **differentiation** (rejecting particular deities and practices) is as historically important as **convergence** (Smith’s dual model).

### Contact, exchange, and what we cannot see

Trade routes linked the Levant to Egypt and Mesopotamia; Egyptian and Hittite empires alternately dominated Canaan in the Late Bronze Age (ca. 1550–1200 BCE). **Well established:** Late Bronze Canaan was a patchwork of city-kingdoms with Egyptian oversight, reflected in the Amarna letters (14th century BCE).

The **Late Bronze collapse** (ca. 1200–1150 BCE)—destructions, drought, migration, shrinkage of trade—is **well established** as a watershed. Iron Age polities that follow, including highland Israel, emerge in a transformed landscape.

For **early Israel specifically**, archaeology cannot yet paint a detailed religious portrait of every village shrine in the 12th century BCE. We infer from later texts, later material culture, comparative religion, and a handful of inscriptions. **Unknown / speculative:** the precise cult of earliest highland settlers before they articulated a distinct Yahwistic identity.

## B. The emergence of ancient Israelite religion

### The first whisper of the name “Israel”

In the fifth year of Pharaoh **Merneptah** (ca. 1207 BCE), a royal stela celebrates victories in Canaan. Near the end, a poetic line lists peoples and city-states laid waste. One entry is **Israel**, written with a determinative suggesting a people or socio-ethnic group rather than a fortified city (**widely accepted interpretation**; Hasel, “Israel in the Merneptah Stela,” *BASOR* 296).

**Well established:** this is the **earliest undisputed extra-biblical mention** of Israel. **Contested:** an earlier possible reference on a Berlin statue pedestal (ca. 1400 BCE) read by Görg, van der Veen, and Theis as “Israel”—many Egyptologists dispute the reading.

The Merneptah stela does **not** tell us what Israel believed. It locates a group in Canaan that Egyptians recognized as significant enough to name—a people associated with grain/agricultural devastation language in the poem (**widely accepted interpretation**; Hasel). That already complicates a purely nomadic “just arrived” picture at the moment of first attestation.

### Models of Israel’s emergence

Biblical narrative in Joshua and Judges describes conquest or gradual takeover of Canaan. For much of the 20th century, **William F. Albright** and **John Bright** harmonized archaeology with a modified conquest/infiltration model. **Since the 1980s–1990s**, survey archaeology in the central highlands has shifted the center of gravity.

| Model | Core claim | Current standing |
|--------|-----------|------------------|
| Military conquest | Israel enters Canaan and destroys Canaanite cities | **Contested**; largely rejected for lack of widespread destruction layers matching Joshua |
| Peaceful infiltration (Alt) | Pastoralists gradually settle | **Contested**; partially absorbed into highland settlement model |
| Social revolution (Gottwald) | Marginal Canaanites revolt | **Contested**; influential but not dominant |
| Peasant withdrawal | Oppressed villagers flee lowlands | **Contested** |
| **Highland settlement / indigenous origins** | New Iron I villages in hills; ethnic Israel emerges in place | **Widely accepted interpretation** with internal debate (Finkelstein, Dever, Faust, Stager) |

**Widely accepted interpretation:** Iron Age I (ca. 1200–1000 BCE) sees **population growth in the central highlands**, new village architecture (four-room house, collared-rim jars), and **absence of pig bones** in some assemblages interpreted as a dietary marker—none of which alone proves “Israelite,” but together correlate with later self-identification (Finkelstein, *Archaeology of the Israelite Settlement*; Dever, *Who Were the Early Israelites*).

**Contested:** Elizabeth Bloch-Smith and others note that **no single artifact** uniquely labels a site “Israelite” in the 12th–11th centuries. Ethnicity may be a **boundary-maintaining identity** crafted through story, law, and memory—not a box archaeology opens and reads.

### Israelite religion in its Canaanite environment

**Widely accepted interpretation:** early Israelite religion belongs to the **family of West Semitic / Levantine religions**, not to an imported package wholly alien to Canaan (Smith, *Origins of Biblical Monotheism*; Nakhai, *Archaeology and the Religions of Canaan and Israel*).

Evidence includes:

- **Divine names and titles** shared across cultures (*El*, *Baal*, *Asherah* / Athirat).  
- **Cult places**: high places (*bāmôt*), altars, standing stones—features known regionally.  
- **Iconography**: *Contested* but argued by some for a Yahwistic cult with Asherah symbolism (Kuntillet ʿAjrud inscriptions, 8th century BCE—**later** than Israel’s emergence but revealing earlier trajectories).  
- **Biblical polemic** against Baal and Asherah presumes **competition**, not ignorance (**well established** as literary evidence of religious pluralism within Israel).

**Religious tradition:** the Torah presents Yahweh revealing himself to Moses as distinct from all other gods. **Historical reconstruction:** many scholars think **Yahweh** was worshipped among Israel (and perhaps linked to regions south/east of Canaan—Edom/Midian—**contested**; see Exodus 3, Egyptian Topographical lists mentioning *Shasu of Yhw*, interpretation disputed).

### The long road to monotheism

Popular accounts imagine a simple line: **polytheism → monotheism**. Specialists almost uniformly reject that simplicity (**widely accepted interpretation** of the state of the field; see MacDonald, *Deuteronomy and the Meaning of “Monotheism”*).

More plausible stages (**contested** ordering and dates):

1. **Henotheism / monolatry** — Israel worships Yahweh exclusively while other nations have their gods (Exodus 20:3–5 read this way in many historical readings).  
2. **Yahweh’s assimilation of El traits** — convergence of titles (*ʾĒl ʿElyōn*, *ʾĒl ʿOlam*) with Yahweh (**widely accepted interpretation** in Smith’s model).  
3. **Conflict with Baal** — especially in northern kingdom narratives (Elijah on Carmel, 1 Kings 18 — **religious tradition** and literary art; historical event **unknown**).  
4. **Denial of other gods’ reality** — “There is no other” (Deutero-Isaiah; **exilic/post-exilic** in mainstream dating).  

**Contested:** whether **Josiah’s reform** (late 7th century BCE, 2 Kings 22–23) reflects historical centralization or idealized Deuteronomistic writing. **Contested:** whether monotheism is essentially **exilic** (Babylon 587 BCE) as Rainer Albertz argues from Deuteronomistic literature, or has **pre-exilic** roots stronger than that (**see** Edelman and Monotheism volumes; Smith in *Wiley Blackwell Companion*, ch. 15).

**Label carefully:** calling pre-exilic Israel “polytheistic” can mislead if it implies modern paganism; **“Yahwistic with a divine council and tolerated or syncretistic elements”** is closer to many portraits (**widely accepted interpretation**).

### Limits of reconstruction

**Well established:** the Hebrew Bible reached substantially its present form through **centuries of editing** after many events it narrates. **Well established:** using Genesis through Judges as straightforward 2nd-millennium history fails critical tests that once linked patriarchs to Nuzi or Mari (**Thompson**, *Historicity of the Patriarchal Narratives*; **Van Seters**, *Abraham in History and Tradition*).

We can map **plausible environments**; we cannot recover every shrine’s daily offering list from 1100 BCE.

## C. Abraham and the patriarchal traditions

### Three separate questions

Abraham (*ʾAvrāhām*) sits at the center of all three later Abrahamic religions—and at the center of **different** questions historians and believers ask.

1. **What do the texts narrate?** (**Religious tradition** / literary corpus)  
2. **What can historians establish about the origin of those narratives?** (**Historical method**)  
3. **How did later communities interpret Abraham?** (**Reception history**)

This project keeps them separate.

### What the narratives relate

In Genesis 12–25 (within the broader ancestral cycle 12–50), Abraham is called from Mesopotamia to Canaan, receives promises of land and descendants, negotiates with kings, separates from Lot, encounters Melchizedek, hears covenant promises (including circumcision in Genesis 17), and is tested at Moriah (Genesis 22). Hagar and Ishmael, Isaac and Sarah, and the binding of Isaac (*ʿAqedah*) belong to this literary world.

**Religious tradition (Judaism):** Abraham is the first patriarch of the covenant; *Avraham avinu*. **Religious tradition (Christianity):** exemplar of faith (Romans 4; Galatians 3). **Religious tradition (Islam):** *Ibrāhīm*, ḥanīf and builder of the Kaʿba in Mecca in Qur’anic narrative (e.g., Q 2:127–129; 22:26–27)—material **not** in Genesis, showing **branching** tradition.

### Historical origins of the narratives

**Widely accepted interpretation among critical scholars:** Genesis 12–50 is **not** a eyewitness archive from the early second millennium BCE. Arguments that once placed Abraham at Mari or Nuzi have been **dismantled** (Thompson 1974; Van Seters 1975). **Contested:** whether **any** memory of second-millennium conditions survives in reduced form.

**Finkelstein and Römer (2014)** propose a nuanced middle path: some Abraham traditions may echo **Iron Age southern highland** realities and shrine geography (e.g., Hebron/Mamre associations), while other layers reflect **exilic and post-exilic** concerns and even possible **Hellenistic** additions—each layer must be argued, not assumed.

**Unknown / speculative:** a historical individual behind the name. **Religious tradition:** Abraham lived; his age and journeys are real. **Historical method:** cannot verify the call from Ur, the battle of Genesis 14’s coalition, or the names of kings as historical actors (**Van Seters** on Genesis 14’s anachronisms).

### Later interpretation without dismissal

Muslims, Christians, and Jews read Abraham **forward** into their canonical frameworks. That is not “lesser” knowledge—it is **different** knowledge. Levenson’s *Inheriting Abraham* shows how three traditions claim one figure without sharing one story (*religious tradition* and comparative theology).

For the Unified Covenant ledger, Abraham is already a **merge candidate** only at the level of **shared symbol** (trust, rejection of idolatry)—not at the level of **unified biography**.

## D. Covenant, law, prophecy, and justice

These themes become engines of later chapters. Here we introduce their **earliest recoverable layers**, without reading rabbinic, Christian, or Islamic doctrine back into the Late Bronze Age.

### Covenant (*bərît*)

Hebrew *bərît* corresponds to binding agreements—between humans, and between God and people. **Well established:** ancient Near Eastern **treaties** share structural elements with biblical covenant texts: preamble, historical prologue, stipulations, blessings and curses, witnesses (Mendenhall, *Law and Covenant in Israel and the Ancient Near East*; later refinements in Weinfeld; see also chapter in *Covenant: A Vital Element of Reformed Theology*, Brill).

**Widely accepted interpretation:** Deuteronomy’s framing resembles **Hittite vassal treaties** of the 14th–13th centuries BCE in broad outline. **Contested:** whether that resemblance proves **early second-millennium** composition, **7th-century** Josianic composition using archaic forms, or **exilic** adaptation of international rhetoric (Berman; Steymans on Neo-Assyrian parallels; **François**, “Is It Time to Abandon the Hittite Treaty Structure of Deuteronomy?” *JETS* 68.4).

**Well established:** ANE treaties are **political** documents; Israelite covenant texts **theologize** politics—Yahweh as suzerain, Israel as vassal. **Contested:** whether any purely “God-human” covenant without monarchy exists in parallel cultures (most say **no close parallel**).

**Religious tradition:** covenant at Sinai/Mount Horeb defines Israel’s eternal obligation. **Historical reconstruction:** covenant theology likely **develops over centuries**, with Deuteronomy and the Priestly writings representing major codifications (**contested** relative dates of J, E, P, D).

### Divine law and communal obligation

Biblical law collections—Covenant Code (Exodus 20–23), Deuteronomic code, Holiness Code (Leviticus 17–26), Priestly torah—embed **cultic**, **criminal**, and **social** norms: Sabbath, altars, slaves, goring ox, treatment of widows and strangers.

**Well established:** many legal **forms and case patterns** resemble Mesopotamian and Hittite precedents (Roth; Greengus). **Widely accepted interpretation:** Israelite law is **not** a copy but a **selective adaptation** under Yahwistic authority.

**Not yet in this chapter:** full treatment of oral vs. written Torah—that belongs to Chapter 2’s Judaism trajectory.

### Prophecy and revelation

**Mesopotamian prophecy** (Mari tablets, 18th century BCE) shows gods speaking through intermediaries—**well established** as context for Israelite prophecy, not as its source (**contested** degree of influence).

Israelite **classical prophecy** (Amos, Isaiah, Jeremiah—**later** than early Israel) introduces **moral critique of kingship** and **covenant lawsuit** (*rîb*) forms. Early Israel likely knew **charismatic figures** (*nəḇîʾîm*, 1 Samuel 10) and **revelatory oracles** at sanctuaries.

**Religious tradition:** prophets deliver God’s word. **Historical reconstruction:** prophetic books are **edited anthologies**; authentic speech of a historical Amos is **widely accepted**; precise words are **contested**.

### Justice and rulers

ANE kings claimed justice; Israelite tradition **holds rulers accountable** to Yahweh’s torah (later: Nathan against David, Elijah against Ahab). **Widely accepted interpretation:** the **critique of unjust kings** is a distinctive emphasis within Israel’s literature relative to some royal ideologies—without implying Israel always practiced what it preached.

### Collective identity

Israel’s self-understanding ties **people**, **land**, and **God** through story: exodus, wilderness, conquest/settlement—each **contested** historically but **foundational** literarily. Merneptah’s “Israel” proves **external** recognition of a name; internal **meaning** of that name develops later (etymologies linking to *ś-r-r* “struggle” with God in Genesis 32 are **religious tradition** / wordplay).

## E. Texts, transmission, and historical memory

### Composition, transmission, authority, interpretation

Four layers must stay distinct in every later chapter:

1. **Composition** — when and where material was first produced  
2. **Transmission** — copying, editing, combining sources  
3. **Authority** — when a community treats a text as binding  
4. **Interpretation** — how readers derive doctrine and law  

**Well established:** the Pentateuch is **composite**. The classic **Documentary Hypothesis** (J, E, D, P) organized 20th-century research (Wellhausen). **Contested:** whether J and E ever existed as discrete documents; European scholarship often favors **fragmentary** or **supplementary** models (Rendtorff; *A Farewell to the Yahwist?*, Brill 2006).

**Widely accepted interpretation:** **Deuteronomy** has a distinct core; **Priestly** material exists with recognizable vocabulary and theology; **editors** link Genesis to Exodus. **Contested:** almost everything about **dates** (Solomonic J vs. exilic J; pre-exilic P vs. exilic P).

For this project’s **Git analogy** (next section): think of **multiple authors committing to a shared repository over centuries**, with **merge conflicts** resolved by redactors who sometimes preserve both versions (doublets, alternate names for God).

### Major chronological uncertainties

| Issue | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|
| Date of exodus traditions | Anchors origin story; archaeological silence at Pi-Ramesses period **contested** |
| Age of patriarchal narratives | Abraham as 2nd vs. 1st millennium product |
| Deuteronomy’s origin | Josianic vs. exilic |
| Priestly writings | Pre-exilic temple vs. exilic blueprint |
| Final Torah redaction | Persian-period community identity (Ezra-Nehemiah) |

**Unknown / speculative:** a single timeline acceptable to all specialists.

### What early Israel likely “had”

**Contested:** extent of **literacy** in Iron I highlands. **Widely accepted interpretation:** oral tradition and **limited** early writing; major literary fixation **later** (monarchy and after). **Religious tradition:** Torah from Moses at Sinai. **Historical reconstruction:** Mosaic torah as **foundation myth** and legal anchor developed over time—without requiring malicious intent, and without denying possible older legal kernels.

## The historical “Git” analogy

Software **Git** tracks files, branches, merges, and conflicts. The Unified Covenant project uses it **only as a metaphor** for clarity—not because ancient religion worked like a dev team.

| Git concept | Historical analogue (used carefully) |
|-------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Repository** | A cultural-religious complex (e.g., West Semitic Levantine religion) |
| **Commit** | A durable innovation: new ritual, royal reform, literary corpus |
| **Branch** | A community maintaining distinct tradition (Israel vs. Judah; later Judaism vs. Christianity vs. Islam) |
| **Merge** | Synthesis of sources (Pentateuch redaction; Christian use of Septuagint) |
| **Conflict** | Incompatible claims preserved side-by-side (double creation accounts; divergent covenant traditions) |
| **Tag / release** | Moments communities treat a text as canonical (much **later** than this chapter) |

**Inherited “dependencies” (evidence-supported):** treaty forms; El/Baal language; flood and creation motifs; legal case patterns; temple ideology.

**Substantial “refactors”:** Yahweh-alone rhetoric; Deuteronomistic history; Priestly theology; prophetic moralization of kingship.

**Uncertain lineage:** Yahweh’s origins (**contested**); Abraham narratives’ oldest layer (**contested**); whether highland Israel “forked” from a single Canaanite trunk or **assembled** identity from several streams (**widely accepted interpretation:** assembly more than fork).

**Do not assume:** one pristine Abrahamic monotheism at the root. **Do not assume:** later traditions are merely corrupted copies. The metaphor helps the **future synthesis engine** ask: *What is shared upstream? What breaks ABI compatibility? What tests are still failing?*

## Closing: what this chapter enables

Before Judaism, Christianity, or Islam existed as identifiable traditions, the Levant produced **Israelite Yahwism**—embedded in a wider Near Eastern world, visible archaeologically only in fragments, narrated in texts edited long after the Iron Age beginnings. The Merneptah stela gives us a **name**; highland archaeology gives us **settlements**; Ugarit and Mesopotamia give us **comparative grammar**; the Hebrew Bible gives us **memory, law, and argument**—not a transparent diary.

Chapter 2 will follow the development of **Israel and Judah**, the **formation of biblical traditions**, and the path toward **Second Temple Judaism**—the trunk from which Christianity and Islam will later branch in ways this chapter deliberately does not anticipate.

The speculative essay [THE_UNIFIED_COVENANT.md](../THE_UNIFIED_COVENANT.md) imagined Abraham, Moses, and prophets as archetypes for a future merge. This chapter grounds that imagination in **evidence labels**, **disagreement**, and **silence** where silence is honest.
