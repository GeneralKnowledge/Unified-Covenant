# Chapter 2: From Israel to Judaism

*Kingdoms, exile, restoration, and the diverse world of Second Temple Judaism*

---

## How to read this chapter

Chapter 1 left us in the highlands and lowlands of early Iron Age Canaan with a name—**Israel**—on an Egyptian stela and a religious landscape still forming. This chapter follows the **political** and **religious** trajectories that turn Yahwistic Israelite communities into the **Judaism** of the Second Temple period: not one straight line, but overlapping stories of kingdoms, empires, texts, and institutions.

As in Chapter 1, claims use **evidence labels** (**Well established**, **Widely accepted interpretation**, **Contested**, **Religious tradition**, **Unknown / speculative**). The [Unified Covenant](../THE_UNIFIED_COVENANT.md) essay remains unchanged speculative background—not a template for this narrative.

**Chronological frame:** roughly 1000 BCE (emergence of monarchy in biblical narrative) through **70 CE** (Roman destruction of the Second Temple), with composition dates of biblical books often **later** than the events they describe.

---

## Prologue: From Merneptah to a world Rome will rule

When the northern kingdom fell to Assyria and Jerusalem fell to Babylon, no one could have foreseen that survivors and their descendants would gather around **Torah**, **temple**, and **diverse schools of interpretation** under Persian, Hellenistic, and Roman rule. **Religious tradition** presents this as providence; **historical reconstruction** describes institutional adaptation under imperial pressure, textual consolidation, and competing visions of what it meant to be Israel after kingship ended.

The central question: **How did Israelite and Judean religion become the plural Judaism that existed when the first century CE began?**

---

## A. Two kingdoms, related but distinct

### The united monarchy and the split

The Hebrew Bible narrates a **united kingdom** under Saul, David, and Solomon, then a **schism** after Solomon’s death (1 Kings 12). **Contested:** archaeology and chronology do not confirm a Solomonic empire on the scale of the Deuteronomistic history; **widely accepted interpretation** that **Iron IIA** (10th century) sees emerging highland polities, with **Jerusalem** growing significantly only later in some reconstructions (Finkelstein & Silberman, *The Bible Unearthed*; contrast Mazar, Dever on 10th-century Judah).

**Well established** by the 9th century BCE: two kingdoms—**Israel** (capital **Samaria**, richer, more fertile) and **Judah** (capital **Jerusalem**, smaller, highland). The **Mesha Stele** (Moab, ca. 840 BCE) and the **Black Obelisk** of Shalmaneser III (841 BCE, Jehu of Israel) attest Israel and Judah in regional politics (**well established**).

### The Omride court and regional politics

In the 9th century, the **Omrides** (Ahab, Jezebel) aligned Israel with **Phoenician** Tyre and appear in Assyrian records as major Levantine players. Biblical narrative condemns their **Baal** patronage (1 Kings 16–18); whether this reflects **accurate cult policy**, **Judahite polemic**, or both is **contested**. The **Tel Dan stele**, if referring to the “house of David,” suggests Judah’s dynasty was known regionally (**widely accepted interpretation** of the reading, though the phrase itself is **contested** among epigraphers).

Trade, marriage alliances, and warfare constantly **blurred borders**. Religion was never sealed off from **economics and empire**.

### Sanctuaries, kings, priests, and prophets

Both kingdoms shared **Yahweh** as a national god, but neither was religiously uniform.

- **Kingship** sponsored cult, building temples and high places; royal inscriptions elsewhere in the Levant show similar sacral kingship patterns (Chapter 1).  
- **Jerusalem’s temple** became central to **Judah’s** identity; **Bethel**, **Dan**, and other sites mattered in the **north** (1 Kings 12:25–33 — **religious tradition** and literary construction).  
- **Priests** managed sacrifice and ritual; **prophets** (Elijah, Elisha in the north; later Isaiah, Micah in Judah) appear in biblical narrative as critics and interpreters of events (**historical cores contested** per figure).

**Widely accepted interpretation:** **Kuntillet ʿAjrud** (Sinai, ca. 8th century BCE) and **Khirbet el-Qom** inscriptions show Yahweh paired with **Asherah** language in some communities—evidence that “official” later monotheism was not yet universal (**Dever**, *Did God Have a Wife?*; interpretation of readings **contested** but widely discussed).

**Archaeology** reveals local shrines, figurines, and diverse cult practice; **biblical polemic** against “high places” (*bāmôt*) documents later **evaluation**, not neutral reporting (**well established** as literary-historical observation).

### Political identity and religious identity

Being “Israelite” or “Judean” involved **kinship, land, king, and god**, but the mix differed north and south. The north had more exposure to Phoenician trade and Aramean pressure; Judah faced Philistine and Edomite neighbors and, later, direct Assyrian threat.

**Contested:** whether Yahweh worship was **the same institution** in both kingdoms. Shared language and stories suggest overlap; divergent royal policies (e.g., Ahab and Jezebel’s Baal patronage in 1 Kings 16 — **religious tradition**) suggest **competition among cults** within elites.

**Limits of evidence:** inscriptions are elite; biblical books are **Judahite** in final form and **hostile** to much northern history (2 Kings 17’s verdict on Samaria). Popular religion must be inferred from **material culture** and **law/prophetic texts**, not assumed uniform.

| Dimension | Israel (north) | Judah (south) |
|-----------|----------------|---------------|
| Capital | Samaria | Jerusalem |
| Economic base | Agriculture, trade routes | Highlands, limited expansion |
| Extra-biblical attestation | Mesha, Assyrian annals | Siloam inscription (late 8th c.), LMLK seals |
| Biblical portrayal | “Sinful” northern kings | Davidic dynasty idealized |
| Cult centers | Multiple (Bethel, Dan, etc. in text) | Jerusalem temple emphasized |

## B. Assyria, the fall of Israel, and the survival of Judah

### The Assyrian age

From the 9th century, **Neo-Assyria** expanded westward. Israel paid tribute; revolt brought devastation. **Well established:** Assyrian campaigns transformed Levantine politics; deportation and resettlement were **standard imperial policy** (Becking, *The Fall of Samaria*).

### Fall of Samaria (722/720 BCE)

2 Kings 17 attributes Samaria’s fall to **Shalmaneser V**, with **Sargon II** claiming credit in Assyrian inscriptions—a **contradiction** resolved by many as **two related events**: capture under Shalmaneser (723/722) and renewed suppression under Sargon (720) (**contested** but influential: Tadmor, Becking; recent discussion in Radner, Hasegawa, Levin, eds., *The Last Days of the Kingdom of Israel*, BZAW 511).

**Well established:** the northern kingdom ceased to exist as an independent polity; **Samaria** became an Assyrian province. **Widely accepted interpretation:** significant **deportations** of Israelites and **importation** of other peoples into the region (2 Kings 17:6, 24; Assyrian administrative texts).

**Do not overstate:** northerners did not vanish from history. Some fled south; others remained; **Samaritan** and other traditions preserve northern-lineage claims (**religious tradition** and ongoing scholarly debate). The “**ten lost tribes**” is a **later** trope—not a demographic census (**widely accepted interpretation**).

### Judah after Israel

Judah survived as a **vassal** state, then rebelled under **Hezekiah** (701 BCE siege of Jerusalem — **well established** in Sennacherib’s annals; miraculous deliverance is **religious tradition**). **Widely accepted interpretation:** the fall of the north **shocked** Judean elites and shaped theological interpretation of covenant and survival (Albertz, *History of Israelite Religion*).

Archaeologically, **Lachish** and other sites show Assyrian destruction layers; Jerusalem’s **Broad Wall** and **Siloam tunnel** are linked to Hezekiah’s reign (**widely accepted interpretation**, with dating debates).

## C. Reform, centralisation, and the growth of religious authority

### Hezekiah and Josiah in biblical memory

The **Deuteronomistic History** (Joshua–Kings) presents **Hezekiah** and especially **Josiah** as reforming kings who centralized worship in Jerusalem and purged foreign cult (**2 Kings 18–20; 22–23**). **Contested:** whether these reforms occurred at the **scale** described.

**Nadav Na’aman** argued Hezekiah may have initiated **centralization** after 701 BCE, with Josiah’s reform as a **later literary and programmatic** development building on that memory (*ZAW* 107, 1995; summary in his BASOR-style treatments). **Juha Pakkala** and others argue **cult reforms probably did not happen** as narrated, and that 2 Kings 22–23 is largely **exilic Deuteronomistic** ideology (*One God – One Cult – One Nation*, ed. Kratz & Spieckermann, 2010).

**Archaeology:** Tel **Arad** sanctuary dismantling, **Beersheba** horned altar, **Lachish** gate shrine—each cited for or against reform; **no consensus** (Luppova, “Cultic reforms… in the light of archaeological data,” *Vestnik drevnei istorii* 2024). **Christoph Uehlinger** argues for a **“well-grounded minimum”** of some Josianic cult policy (*in* Grabbe, *Hebrew Bible and History*).

### Deuteronomy and the “book of the law” (622 BCE)

**Religious tradition:** priests found a law book in the temple; Josiah enacted it. **Widely accepted interpretation among critical scholars:** this story **authorizes** Deuteronomic law retroactively; the “discovery” is a **literary device** (cf. skeptical analysis in *Journal of Hebrew Scriptures*).

**Contested dating of Deuteronomy:**

- **Josianic** (late 7th century) — classic alignment with reform.  
- **Exilic/post-exilic** — Pakkala, Römer: Ur-Deuteronomy fits loss of king and temple.  
- **Neo-Assyrian treaty influence** — parallels in Deut 13, 28 (**contested** direct dependence vs. shared scribal culture; Steymans, Koch, Polk).

**Why it matters:** if Deuteronomy is **exilic**, Josiah’s reform is **narrative** preparing readers for loss of monarchy, not a live royal program. If **Josianic**, Judah briefly pursued **exclusive Jerusalem worship** before Babylon ended the experiment.

### Prophets and power

**Amos**, **Hosea** (north), **Isaiah**, **Micah** (Judah) in 8th century texts voice **justice**, **covenant lawsuit**, and **critique of elites**. **Contested:** authentic ipsissima verba vs. extensive later editing. **Well established:** prophetic books were **shaped** in exilic/post-exilic periods regardless of earlier cores.

Prophetic literature **evaluates** kings; it does not prove reforms succeeded on the ground.

### Exclusive Yahweh worship vs. practice

Biblical law and prophets demand **exclusive loyalty**; inscriptions and figurines suggest **diverse practice** persisted (**widely accepted interpretation**). The tension is **data**, not a failure of method: texts often state **norms**; archaeology shows **behavior**.

## D. Babylon, destruction, and exile

### 587/586 BCE

**Well established:** **Nebuchadnezzar II** destroyed **Jerusalem** and its **temple**; **Judah’s monarchy** ended. **Well established:** **deportations** to Babylonia (2 Kings 25; Babylonian administrative context — **widely accepted interpretation**).

**Widely accepted interpretation:** only a **minority** of Judeans were deported; many **remained** in Judah and neighboring regions (Hans Barstad, *The Myth of the Empty Land*; Grabbe on exile terminology). “Exile” is simultaneously **historical experience** for deportees and **theological category** for all who lost temple and king (**Grabbe**, *Judaic Religion in the Second Temple Period*, introduction).

### Life in Babylon and identity

**Cuneiform tablets** from **Al-Yahudu** and related archives document Judeans farming, naming, and living under Babylonian administration (**well established** for exilic presence; Pearce & Wunsch, *Documents of Judean Exiles*). Names such as **Yahu-aziz** and **Yahu-kin** show the god **Yahu/Yahweh** embedded in Babylonian-documented identity— not proof of synagogue worship or Torah reading, but evidence that deportees were not instantly absorbed without trace.

**Contested:** how far deportees maintained distinct **covenant** identity vs. assimilation. Some scholars emphasize **economic integration**; others highlight **communal cohesion** visible in naming and family structure. Both may be true in different households.

Meanwhile, those who **remained in Judah** lived among ruins, unsettled borders, and **competing claims** to land (Barstad). The “exile” as **theological totality**—all Israel punished, all must repent—can **outrun** the demography of deportation yet still **shape** scripture that survivors and deportees later share.

Theological developments associated with exile include:

- **Collective punishment** and **repentance** themes (Lamentations; Deuteronomistic history).  
- **Divine sovereignty** without land or king (Ezekiel; Deutero-Isaiah — **contested** dating and authorship).  
- **Explicit monotheizing** rhetoric (“besides me there is no god,” Isaiah 45 — **widely accepted interpretation** as exilic or post-exilic composition).

**Contested:** whether **monotheism** was **born** in exile or **accelerated** there (Smith; Albertz; see Chapter 1 ledger). Exile **did not** instantly produce rabbinic Judaism or a closed **Tanakh**.

### Prophetic and historical traditions

**Deuteronomistic History** likely reached substantial form in **exilic** Judah/Babylon (**widely accepted interpretation**). It interprets Assyrian and Babylonian disasters as **covenant consequences**—**theology** encoding **historical trauma**.

## E. Return, Persian rule, and the rebuilding of a community

### Cyrus and the Persian empire

**Well established:** **Cyrus** captured Babylon in **539 BCE**; the **Persian empire** incorporated **Yehud** (the province of Judah). The **Cyrus Cylinder** describes restoring cults and repatriating peoples in **Mesopotamian** context—it **does not mention** Jews or Jerusalem (**well established**; scholarly summary in Tiemeyer, “The Cyrus Cylinder in Biblical Scholarship”).

**Contested:** authenticity of **Ezra 1**’s Hebrew and Aramaic **decrees**. **Widely accepted interpretation** (Grabbe): a **general policy** allowing some returns and temple rebuilding is plausible; the **biblical decree text** is not straightforwardly authentic. **Return** was likely a **“trickle”** over decades, not a single mass migration of 42,360 (Ezra 2 — **religious tradition** with demographic debate; archaeology suggests modest early Persian-period settlement).

### Temple, priesthood, and Torah

**Well established:** the **Second Temple** was rebuilt (completed traditionally **515 BCE**; dedication narratives in Ezra 6 — **religious tradition** with historical core). **Priests** (Zadokite lineage) and **governors** (Zerubbabel, Nehemiah in narrative) structured **Yehud**.

**Ezra** and **Nehemiah** — **contested** chronology (some scholars reverse or separate their missions; Grabbe, *A History of the Jews and Judaism in the Second Temple Period*). **Religious tradition:** Ezra “reads the Torah” (Nehemiah 8) and defines community. **Historical reconstruction:** this scene may reflect **later** authorization of **Torah** as public constitution (**contested** date: 5th vs. 4th century BCE).

**Composition vs. authority:**

| Stage | Question |
|-------|----------|
| Composition | When were Pentateuchal sources combined? (**Contested** — pre-exilic P vs. exilic/Persian redaction) |
| Transmission | Scribal copying and harmonization |
| Sacred status | When was material treated as **Scripture**? (**Widely accepted interpretation:** Persian period important; **contested** details) |
| Authority | When did **Torah** govern **marriage, Sabbath, temple** as community charter? (**Contested**) |

**Do not conflate** Persian-period **Yehud** with **rabbinic Judaism** (centuries later). Persian administration used **local elites**; **Torah** may have functioned partly as **imperial-friendly** self-rule charter (**contested**; Frei, “Persian imperial authorization” thesis debated in Grabbe and others).

### Diaspora beginnings

Judean communities in **Babylon**, **Egypt**, and elsewhere show **diversity** before Hellenism (**widely accepted interpretation**).

The **Elephantine** papyri (5th century BCE) are especially instructive: a Judean garrison on the Nile worshipped **Yahu** in a local temple, corresponded with officials in **Jerusalem** and **Samaria**, and knew **Passover**—yet their religion did not match later **Deuteronomic** exclusivism (they had **other gods** in oath formulae in some documents, **contested** interpretation). When their temple was destroyed, they petitioned Jerusalem for rebuild permission— revealing **plural centers of authority** and **negotiation** over what “proper” worship required.

Such evidence prevents us from projecting **one** Persian-period Judaism backward or forward unchanged.

## F. Exodus, Sinai, and the memory of origins

Chapter 1 deferred these; Chapter 2 must address them because **Persian and Second Temple Judaism** treats **Exodus–Sinai** as **foundational memory**, regardless of historical reconstruction.

### 1. Historical event behind Exodus?

**Positions (all contested in details):**

| Position | Claim | Typical evidence cited |
|----------|--------|-------------------------|
| **Minimalist / revisionist** | No mass exodus; Israel largely indigenous | Archaeology of Transjordan/entry routes; lack of Egyptian records |
| **Moderate** | Literary growth around **small-scale** events or memories | Hyksos expulsion analogies (**contested**); Egyptian sojourn motifs |
| **Maximalist / selective** | Substantial 13th-c. BCE departure possible | Pi-Ramesses (Qantir), Egyptian geography (Hoffmeier, *Israel in Egypt*; *JETS* 50/2) |

**Well established:** **no Egyptian** royal inscription records Israel’s exodus as biblical narrative describes. **Widely accepted interpretation:** absence of evidence is **not** proof of absence, but forces caution.

**Finkelstein & Silberman:** exodus tradition may echo **Hyksos** memory and **7th-century** anti-Egyptian politics (*The Bible Unearthed*). **Hoffmeier:** Ramesside setting fits **some** toponyms and labor systems. **Neither** consensus.

**Unknown / speculative:** population numbers, route, and **identification of Pharaoh**.

### 2. Sinai covenant traditions

**Religious tradition:** law given at Sinai/Horeb to Moses. **Historical reconstruction:** covenant and law texts **develop** over centuries; Hittite/Assyrian **treaty forms** inform Deuteronomy (**widely accepted interpretation** of form, **contested** date). **J**, **E**, **P** strands place revelation at Sinai with different emphases (**contested** source model; see Chapter 1).

### 3. When did Exodus become foundational?

**Widely accepted interpretation:** by **Persian period** and certainly **Second Temple**, Passover and exodus motifs **structure** calendar and identity (Exodus 12–13; Deuteronomy 16). **Contested:** whether **Northern** Israel emphasized exodus equally before Assyrian fall.

### 4. Liberation, law, land, covenant in later interpretation

Deuteronomistic and Priestly traditions **bind** release from Egypt to **obedience**, **land**, and **temple**. **Exilic** writers reuse exodus as **hope pattern** (second exodus in Isaiah 40–55). This **interpretive chain** is **well established** literarily; it does not prove each link as ancient history.

### 5. Authority despite historical dispute

Communities treat exodus as **charter myth**—foundational whether or not cameras would have recorded it. **Historical method** separates **charter function** from **event verification** without declaring charter **unimportant** (Assmann’s “counterhistory” language is **interpretive**, not a label of falsity).

## G. The Second Temple world

### Temple, priesthood, sacrifice

Under Persians, **Ptolemies**, **Seleucids**, and **Hasmoneans**, the **Jerusalem temple** remained the **symbolic center** for most Jews, even when **diaspora** communities rarely saw it. **Priests** controlled sacrifice; **Torah** increasingly regulated **pure/impure**, **Sabbath**, **festivals**.

**Religious tradition:** eternal covenant centered on temple. **Historical fact:** temple **destroyed 586**, rebuilt **515**, **profaned and rededicated** 164 BCE (Hanukkah narrative), **destroyed again 70 CE**.

### Hellenism and the Septuagint

**Well established:** **Alexander** (332 BCE) and successors spread **Greek** language and culture. **Well established:** **Septuagint** (Greek Torah/Pentateuch, 3rd–2nd century BCE Egypt) enabled **diaspora** Jews to read scripture in **Greek**—a **major fork** in transmission (later Christian use is **outside** this chapter’s scope except as transmission fact).

### Maccabees, Hasmoneans, Rome

**Well established:** **Antiochus IV Epiphanes** attempted to suppress Judean practices and assert **Seleucid** control over the temple; **Hasmonean** leaders led **armed resistance** (1 Maccabees — **religious tradition** with historical core; **widely accepted interpretation** that Seleucid crisis was real).

The **rededication** of the temple (164 BCE) became **Hanukkah** memory—celebrating **restoration** of altar and law. **Hasmonean** rulers later took the **high priesthood** and then **kingship**, expanding territory through alliance and conquest. That fusion of **priest + king + warrior** had no simple precedent in ideal Deuteronomistic kingship; it illustrates how **political survival** reshaped **religious authority**.

**Pharisees**, **Sadducees**, and others debated **Hellenization**: how much Greek education, gymnasium culture, and foreign customs a faithful Judean could absorb (2 Maccabees; Josephus). **Not every Jew** chose the same answer.

**Pompey** entered Jerusalem **63 BCE**, intervening in a Hasmonean succession dispute; Rome eventually placed **Herod the Great** as client king. Herod’s **massive temple expansion** made Jerusalem a marvel of the Roman East— even as many Judeans resented his **Idumean** origin and Roman dependency.

Under **direct Roman prefects** in Judea (from 6 CE) and **client kings** elsewhere, **taxation**, **pilgrimage crowds**, and **messianic speculation** created a pressure cooker. **Galilee**, where many Jews lived under Herodian tetrarchs, was **not** identical to Judea administratively, yet shared **Torah**, **festivals**, and **rumors of prophets**.

### Diversity: not every Jew in a “sect”

**Josephus** names **Pharisees**, **Sadducees**, **Essenes** (*Antiquities* 18.11–25). **Widely accepted interpretation** (Grabbe, Cohen, Schiffman):

- **Pharisees:** oral tradition alongside written Torah; **resurrection**; popular teachers.  
- **Sadducees:** priestly elites; **written Torah** emphasis; **no resurrection** (Acts 23:8 reflects tradition).  
- **Essenes:** separatist purity communities; **Qumran** often linked (**contested** identity of Qumran).

**Well established:** **most Jews** were **not** sect members—shared **Sabbath**, **circumcision**, **pilgrimage**, **dietary customs**, **synagogue** life emerging (**Grabbe**, vol. 1, on sectarian minorities).

### Synagogues, scripture, and daily Judaism

Outside Jerusalem, **synagogues** (Greek *proseuchē* / gathering places) developed as venues for **prayer, reading, and community**— especially in diaspora cities. Origins are **contested** (some trace pre-exilic roots; **widely accepted interpretation** emphasizes **post-exilic** and **Hellenistic** growth). Synagogue Judaism did **not** replace temple sacrifice for those who could reach Jerusalem; it **supplemented** worship for many Jews.

**Scrolls** of Torah and Prophets circulated among communities; **interpretation** (midrashic habits later codified by rabbis) began long before the **Mishnah**. **Wisdom** literature (Proverbs, Job, Ecclesiastes, Sirach) shows **Torah-adjacent** ethics without identical vocabulary—another face of Second Temple **pluralism**.

### Apocalyptic, messiah, resurrection

**Daniel** (likely **2nd century BCE**) introduces **resurrection** explicitly in Hebrew canon (**widely accepted interpretation** of dating). **Apocalyptic** literature (1 Enoch traditions, etc.) flourishes (**contested** origins and communities). Visions of **angels**, **cosmic combat**, and **final judgment** offered hope when **empires** seemed permanent.

**Messianic** expectations varied: **Davidic** king, **priestly** deliverer, **prophetic** figure—**not** one uniform “Jewish messiah checklist” (**contested** generalizations; Collins, *The Apocalyptic Imagination*). Some groups withdrew to **desert** communities (Qumran); others engaged **urban** politics. **Zealots** and related currents tied **God’s kingdom** to **anti-Roman** action—a strategy other Jews rejected as suicidal provocation.

### Arrival at the first century CE

By **ca. 30 CE**, the Jewish world included:

- **Jerusalem** temple elite and **pilgrims**  
- **Galilean** and **Judean** villages under Roman administration  
- **Diaspora** from Rome to Alexandria  
- **Competing** legal and eschatological schools  

This is the **environment** in which the **Jesus movement** will appear—the **next** chapter’s subject, not developed here.

**Second Temple Judaism** names a **period** (roughly exilic trauma through 70 CE), **not** a denomination. Rabbinic Judaism **emerges after** 70—continuity and rupture (**contested** how to tell that story).

## The historical “Git” metaphor (continued)

Use sparingly:

- **Merge:** Northern prophetic traditions and southern Davidic theology **combined** in Judah after 722—**not** a git merge with clean diff, but **literary** incorporation in prophetic books and Kings.  
- **Branch:** **Samaritan** community preserves **Torah-focused** tradition with **different** holy mountain (Gerizim) — real **fork**, not heresy in their self-understanding.  
- **Cherry-pick:** Deuteronomistic historians **select** kings and events to teach **covenant** theology.  
- **Commit message vs. code:** Josiah narrative **messages** reform; **archaeology** may not compile the same binary.

No ancient scribe ran `git`; the metaphor clarifies **layered authorship** for the later synthesis engine.
