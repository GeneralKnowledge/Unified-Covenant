# Chapter 3: The Emergence of Christianity

*From a Jewish movement around Jesus of Nazareth to diverse Christian communities in the early second century CE*

---

## How to read this chapter

[Chapter 2](./02-from-israel-to-judaism.md) ended in **first-century Judea**: Roman rule, a functioning **Second Temple**, and **Jewish diversity**—Pharisees, Sadducees, apocalyptic groups, diaspora synagogues, and ordinary Jews who belonged to no sect. Christianity did not appear in that world as a finished religion. It **emerged** as some Jews—and soon gentiles—came to believe that **Jesus of Nazareth**, executed under Roman authority, had been **raised** and **exalted** by Israel’s God.

This chapter is **historical reconstruction**, not theological proof or disproof. [THE_UNIFIED_COVENANT.md](../THE_UNIFIED_COVENANT.md) remains a separate speculative essay and is **not** project doctrine.

Evidence labels match earlier chapters: **Well established**, **Widely accepted interpretation**, **Contested**, **Religious tradition**, **Unknown / speculative**.

**Central question:** How did a Jewish movement centered on Jesus become recognizably **Christian** communities by the early second century CE—without assuming that outcome was inevitable?

---

## A. The world into which Jesus was born

### Roman Judea and Herodian Galilee

By the early first century CE, **Judea** and **Galilee** were embedded in the **Roman imperial system**. After Pompey’s intervention (63 BCE) and Herod the Great’s client kingdom, **direct Roman prefects** governed Judea from **6 CE**, while **Antipas** and later rulers held Galilee and neighboring territories. Taxes, roads, and military presence linked villages to an empire that tolerated local cult **if public order held**.

**Well established:** the **Jerusalem Temple** was the economic, ritual, and symbolic center of Jewish life for many Jews worldwide. Pilgrimage festivals (Passover especially) swelled the city’s population and political tension.

**Widely accepted interpretation:** most Jews observed **Torah** in some form—**Sabbath**, **circumcision**, **purity** rules, **dietary** practice, **prayer** toward Jerusalem—while debating **interpretation** (Pharisees vs. Sadducees on oral tradition and resurrection, per Josephus and later rabbinic sources).

### Diversity and expectation

Jews did **not** share one messianic blueprint. Some expected a **Davidic king**; others a **prophetic** figure; apocalyptic texts imagined **divine intervention**, **judgment**, and **restoration**. **Resurrection** of the dead was **accepted** in some circles (Pharisees; Daniel 12) and **denied** in others (Sadducees — Acts 23:8 reflects **religious tradition** echoed by Josephus).

**Greek** language and **Hellenistic** culture permeated cities and diaspora; Galilee and Judea were **bilingual** borderlands. Jesus’ movement will arise **inside** this matrix—not as an imported “non-Jewish” philosophy.

Economic life in Galilee turned on **agriculture**, **fishing**, and **taxation** funneled through local elites and Roman agents. Banditry, debt, and land loss appear in both literary sources and archaeological surveys of rural settlement. When later Gospels speak of **“the poor”** and **debt forgiveness**, they touch nerves in a province where **covenantal language** and **imperial extraction** overlapped. None of that proves any single saying came from Jesus’ lips, but it explains why **kingdom of God** preaching could sound simultaneously **spiritual** and **material** to first-century listeners.

## B. The historical Jesus

### Sources and limits

Our primary sources are **Mark**, **Matthew**, **Luke**, and **John** (written **decades** after Jesus’ death), **Paul’s letters** (earlier, but focused on belief and communities, not biography), and **Josephus** (partial, debated passages). Historians use **criteria** (multiple attestation, embarrassment, coherence) knowing all methods are **contested** (Meier, *A Marginal Jew*; Allison, *Constructing Jesus*; Dunn, *Jesus Remembered*).

**Well established** among mainstream historians: **Jesus existed**, was **Jewish**, operated in **Galilee**, and was executed under **Roman prefect Pontius Pilate** (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44; Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.63–64 with debated wording; Gospel passion narratives).

**Contested:** year of birth (Herod vs. census traditions in Luke 2); length of ministry; exact itinerary.

### John the Baptist, teacher, prophet

**Well established** (multiple attestation: Gospels, Josephus *Ant.* 18.116–119): **John the Baptist** preached **repentance** and **immersion** in the Jordan region; Jesus was **associated** with John before his independent activity (**widely accepted interpretation**).

Jesus is portrayed as **teacher** (parables, Torah debate), **healer/exorcist** (typical of charismatic holy men in the period—Vermes, *Jesus the Jew*), and **proclaimer of God’s kingdom** (*malkhut shamayim*). **Contested** emphasis: **apocalyptic prophet** expecting imminent divine rule (Sanders, Fredriksen) vs. **wisdom teacher** with social critique vs. **hybrid** (Allison).

### Law, temple, and followers

Debates in the Synoptics place Jesus in **dispute** over **Sabbath**, **purity**, and **Temple** authority—whether he **opposed** Torah or **reinterpreted** it is **contested** (Sanders argues Jesus did not abolish law; some passages suggest radical table fellowship). He gathered **followers**, including **Twelve** (symbolic of Israel’s tribes—**religious tradition** with political overtones) and **women** (Mary Magdalene, Joanna, others in Luke 8:1–3—**historical presence** of women supporters **widely accepted interpretation**, details **contested**).

**Do not assume** Jesus used later terms **“Christ”** or **“Son of God”** as Nicene theology does. **Contested:** how much **messianic** identity he claimed vs. what disciples **projected** after Easter.

E. P. Sanders’ influential portrait treats Jesus as an **eschatological prophet** announcing God’s **impending kingdom** and **restoration of Israel**, while embodying a **radical ethic of inclusion** at table (Sanders, *The Historical Figure of Jesus*). Dale Allison emphasizes **apocalyptic** threads even in “ethical” sayings. Geza Vermes places Jesus among **charismatic Galilean holy men** (*ḥasidim* or “devout ones”) whose healings and exorcisms signaled **God’s present action**. These models overlap more than older “liberal Jesus vs. apocalyptic Jesus” debates admitted; they disagree mainly on **emphasis** and **imminence** of eschatology.

**Parables** (Mark 4; Matthew 13; Luke 15) are **literary compositions** in Greek (or translated from Aramaic—**contested**) that compress social conflict into **story**. Historians use them cautiously: they reveal **early Christian memory** of how Jesus taught, and possibly **authentic patterns** (kingdom as surprise, reversal of status, critique of religious elites), but rarely provide **verifiable datable events**.

## C. Crucifixion under Rome

### Jerusalem, temple incident, execution

**Widely accepted interpretation:** Jesus’ **final visit** to Jerusalem (Passover context in Mark/Matthew/Luke) ended in arrest and **crucifixion**—Roman capital punishment for sedition and troublemakers.

The **Temple incident** (overturning tables, prophecy of destruction) is **religious tradition** in all four Gospels with **differing** timing (Synoptics: final week; John: early ministry). Historians debate whether one **historical disturbance** underlies the accounts or **theological dramatization** (**contested**).

**Well established:** **Pontius Pilate** held authority to execute; crucifixion was **Roman**, not a Sanhedrin penalty. **Gospel narratives** involve **chief priests** and crowds; these reflect **specific actors** and **literary choices**, not collective guilt of **Jews as a people**—a distinction the chapter must preserve because later **anti-Jewish** readings are **historical interpretations**, not facts.

**Josephus** (*Ant.* 18.63–64, **Testimonium Flavianum**) mentions Jesus’ crucifixion under Pilate; the passage likely includes **Christian interpolations** (**widely accepted interpretation**; minority argues for fuller authenticity—Schmidt and others). **Tacitus** (*Ann.* 15.44, ca. 116 CE) reports Christ’s execution under Pilate during Tiberius—external but **late** and brief.

**Unknown / speculative:** exact sequence from arrest to cross; whether Roman or temple authorities drove the immediate charge.

Historically, **Rome** cared about **sedition** and **crowd control** during festivals. High-priestly families, appointed and removable by Rome, cared about **temple order** and **collaborative stability**. A preacher hailed by some as **“king”** (Mark 15:26; John 19:12–15) during **Passover** was precisely the kind of figure Pilate could not ignore. **Religious tradition** in John emphasizes **Jewish leadership** demanding crucifixion; **Synoptic** accounts include **crowd** scenes that later readers weaponized against Jews. Historians note that **Gospel writers** wrote after **70 CE**, when **Jewish–Christian** relations were strained and **Roman** power was the unchallengeable fact on the ground—shaping how blame is distributed in narrative without mapping cleanly onto **legal responsibility** in 30 CE.

Paula Fredriksen and others argue that **messianic acclamation** around Jesus in Jerusalem, even if limited, was enough to trigger **Roman** execution regardless of **Sanhedrin** debates. That reconstruction keeps **agency** with **imperial violence** while explaining why **temple elites** might have preferred Jesus’ removal as a **disturber**.

## D. Resurrection belief and the birth of the movement

Three questions must stay separate:

1. **What did earliest followers believe and proclaim?**  
2. **What can historians reconstruct about origins of those beliefs?**  
3. **Can historical method verify the theological claim that God raised Jesus?**

### Earliest testimony

**Widely accepted interpretation:** 1 Corinthians 15:3–7 preserves a **pre-Pauline** formula Paul **received** and **passed on**—Christ died, was buried, raised on the third day, appeared to Cephas, the Twelve, etc. (MacGregor, *JETS* 49/2; discussion in Hurtado). **Contested:** how many verses belong to the original formula vs. Pauline expansion.

**Religious tradition:** God raised Jesus bodily; empty tomb in Gospels. **Historical reconstruction:** followers experienced **appearances** (visionary, physical, or both in report)—and interpreted them through **Jewish resurrection** hope. **Historical methods do not adjudicate** supernatural causation; they can document **belief**, **chronology**, and **social effects**.

**Well established:** resurrection conviction **launched** the movement’s expansion and reinterpretation of Jesus’ death as **vindication**, not defeat (Hurtado, “Resurrection-Faith and the ‘Historical’ Jesus”).

### Gospels and development

**Mark** (likely earliest Gospel, **contested** date ca. 65–75 CE) ends with empty tomb and fear; **Matthew** and **Luke** add appearances; **John** has distinct appearance scenes. **Empty tomb** narratives are **absent** from Paul’s early formula—**contested** significance (some argue tomb tradition developed later; others argue it was early but not in the creed).

Jewish **resurrection** expected **end-of-age** vindication; proclaiming **one man** raised **in the middle of history** was **novel** and **contested** even among Jews (cf. Acts 23).

Historians who **do not** affirm resurrection theologically still explain its **causal role**: without conviction that God **vindicated** Jesus, the movement likely would not have **persisted** as a **Jewish** renewal group after crucifixion—a shameful death that normally **discredited** messianic claimants (see discussion in Wright, *Resurrection of the Son of God*, and skeptical responses in Ludemann). Alternative naturalistic proposals (grief hallucination, continuation without bodily claims, etc.) remain **contested** and often fail to account for **James**, **Paul**, and **multi-person** appearance lists in the early formula.

The **empty tomb** tradition may have functioned to **dramatize** resurrection for **Gentile** readers unfamiliar with **Jewish** resurrection categories; whether it was **early** Jerusalem belief or **Markan** innovation is **actively debated**. Either way, **appearances** stand at the center of **1 Corinthians 15**, not tomb inspection.

## E. The earliest Jesus movement within Judaism

### Jerusalem and leadership

After crucifixion, followers in **Jerusalem** continued **Temple worship**, **prayer**, and **scriptural interpretation** while proclaiming Jesus as **raised** and **exalted** (**Acts** portrayal—**theological narrative**; Paul’s references to **Peter**, **James**, and the **Jerusalem church** in Galatians 1–2 treated as **early historical evidence** with bias).

**James the brother of Jesus** led the Jerusalem community in many reconstructions (**widely accepted interpretation**; Josephus, *Ant.* 20.200, on his execution). **Contested:** how “Jewish” this community remained in halakhic terms.

### Christological language without Nicene precision

Early titles include **Messiah/Christ**, **Lord** (*kyrios*), **Son of God**, **Son of Man** (Danielic overtones). **Not** all followers held one systematic **ontology** of Jesus. **Widely accepted interpretation** (Hurtado, *Lord Jesus Christ*): **devotional practices**—prayer in Jesus’ name, baptism, *maranatha*, hymnody—show **unprecedented reverence** for a human figure within **Jewish monotheistic** discourse, earlier than Bousset’s late “Hellenistic” model.

**Contested:** whether this devotion equals “divinity” in later creedal sense or **functional** exaltation language (debate continues between Hurtado, Dunn, Ehrman, and others).

**Religious tradition:** Jesus is eternal Word incarnate (John); Synoptics are more **reticent**. **Do not** read Nicene **Trinity** into the 30s CE.

Luke–Acts presents a **harmonious** Jerusalem church (**Acts 2–5**), but Paul’s letters hint at **friction** (Galatians 2; Philippians 3). **Acts 6–8** narrates **Hellenists** vs. **Hebrews** and the **Stephen** conflict—**literary theology** of Christian origins that may preserve **memory** of **cultural division** within Jesus-followers. **Historical reconstruction** must weigh **Paul**, **Acts**, and **later** patristic silence on James’ community without treating any one source as complete.

**Worship** likely remained **Jewish** in form—scripture reading, prayer, blessing God—while adding **thanksgiving for Jesus** and **baptism in his name**. That pattern is what Hurtado calls **“binitarian” devotion**: God remains **one**, yet Jesus receives **cultic honor** normally reserved for **divine** figures in Jewish idiom (**contested** whether this violates strict monotheism as later rabbis defined it).

## F. Paul, the gentile mission, and the question of Torah

### Paul as source

Seven **undisputed** Pauline letters (1 Thessalonians, Galatians, 1–2 Corinthians, Romans, Philippians, Philemon) are **well established** as authentic; others (Ephesians, Pastorals, etc.) are **contested**.

Paul was a **Jew** (“of the tribe of Benjamin,” Philippians 3:5) who persecuted the Jesus movement, then **changed allegiance** (Galatians 1–2 — his account). **Contested:** psychological and visionary details; **historical fact** of dramatic reversal **widely accepted**.

### Gentiles and circumcision

**Central historical process:** inclusion of **gentiles** without full **proselyte conversion** (circumcision, full Torah observance).

In **Galatians** (dated **contested**—South Galatia **ca. 48 CE** before Acts 15 council vs. North Galatia **mid-50s**), Paul insists gentiles are **justified by faith in Christ**, not by becoming Jews. **Acts 15** narrates a **Jerusalem council** agreeing gentiles need not be circumcised but should observe some limits (Acts 15:20); **Paul’s Galatians 2** describes **tension** with **Peter** at Antioch over table fellowship.

**Do not caricature:** Second Temple **Judaism** was not uniformly “salvation by works”; Paul argues within **Jewish** categories about **Torah’s scope** for gentiles in the **messianic age** (Sanders, *Paul and Palestinian Judaism*).

**Widely accepted interpretation:** Paul’s **mission** created **networks** of mixed communities across the **eastern Mediterranean**—Corinth, Ephesus, Philippi, Rome (letter), etc.—with **local** variation.

### Continuity or rupture?

**Contested:** Paul as **reformer within Judaism** vs. **founder of a new trajectory**. His letters show he still valued **Israel’s scriptures** and collection for **Jerusalem poor** (Romans 15). Yet gentile **non-observance** of full Torah became a **new norm** in his churches—**emerging distinction** with far-reaching effects.

Romans **9–11** preserves Paul’s ** anguish** over Israel’s role in God’s plan—evidence against a simplistic “replacement theology” reading of Paul in his own century, even if later Christians read him that way. **Philippians 3** and **2 Corinthians 11** show Paul **competing** with other **apostles** and **“super-apostles”** for **authority**—the Jesus movement was **not** administratively unified.

On **justification**, Paul’s Greek terms (*dikaiosynē*, *pistis*) translate **Jewish** debates about **covenant membership** and **eschatological vindication**, not modern **Protestant–Catholic** polemics. **Works of law** (*erga nomou*) in Galatians likely target **specific boundary markers** (circumcision, food tables) rather than **ethical obedience** as such (Sanders; Dunn, *The New Perspective on Paul*)—**contested** but essential to avoid caricature.

The **Jerusalem meeting** in Galatians 2 and Acts 15 should be read **side by side**, not merged. Paul claims **approval** of his gentile mission; Acts adds **James’** decree with **food** restrictions—possibly **later compromise** traditions. Historians use both to map **negotiation**, not **instant consensus**.

## G. Gospels, memory, and competing accounts

### Composition and context

| Gospel | Approx. date (contested) | Distinctive features |
|--------|-------------------------|----------------------|
| Mark | ca. 65–75 CE | Brief, apocalyptic, suffering Messiah |
| Matthew | ca. 80–90 CE | Torah emphasis, church instruction |
| Luke–Acts | ca. 85–95 CE | Universal scope, Roman political framing |
| John | ca. 90–110 CE | High christology, “I am” discourses |

**Widely accepted interpretation:** **Mark** used by **Matthew** and **Luke**; **Q** hypothesized for shared non-Markan material (**contested**). **John** largely independent.

**70 CE** destruction of **Temple** shapes theology (Mark 13; Luke 21) but **does not explain every feature** of Gospels (**contested** dating of sources).

### History and theology together

Gospels are **faith documents** preserving **memory** through **interpretation**. Contradictions (genealogies, timing of Temple act, resurrection details) signal **multiple streams**, not automatic falsity of all events. Historians infer **probable cores** (baptism, teaching, followers, crucifixion) while analyzing **theological shaping**.

**Acts** links Peter and Paul narratives but **harmonizes** and **theologizes** (compare Galatians 1–2). **Use Acts** for **early Christian self-understanding** and **some** historical data—not as equal witness to Paul’s letters on **conflict** episodes.

Non-canonical gospels (e.g., **Gospel of Thomas**, **Gospel of Peter**) show **alternative** Jesus traditions circulating before **canon** closure. Thomas’ **sayings** collection may preserve **early** material independent of Synoptics (**contested**); it also lacks **passion narrative**, illustrating how **genres** diverged. **Pseudepigrapha** and **infancy gospels** (later) show **continued** imaginative expansion—evidence for **memory’s creativity**, not for uniform **oral** stability.

**Oral tradition** models (form criticism; Bailey’s Middle Eastern village memory; Dunn’s **“impact”** criteria) explain how **stories** evolved while retaining **identifiable cores**. Complete **skepticism** (Jesus never said anything recoverable) and complete **harmonization** (Gospels are court transcripts) both fail **widely accepted interpretation** of current scholarship.

## H. From Jewish movement to distinct communities

### 70 CE and after

**Well established:** Romans destroyed **Jerusalem** and the **Second Temple** in **70 CE** during the First Jewish Revolt. **Effects:** sacrifice-centered Judaism **transformed**; Jesus-followers and other Jews **renegotiated** identity without temple.

**Widely accepted interpretation:** many Jesus-followers were still **Jewish** in self-understanding for decades—keeping **Sabbath**, **Torah**, synagogue participation in some places (**contested** extent).

### Gradual parting of ways

**Do not** assume one **council** or **expulsion** split Judaism and Christianity everywhere. **Boyarin**, *Border Lines*: **heresiologists** (Christian and rabbinic) **drew borders** in the 2nd–4th centuries; before that, **hybrid** practices and beliefs crossed lines (**widely accepted interpretation** among many late-antiquity scholars, **contested** by others who emphasize earlier separation).

**Ebionites**, **Nazarenes**, and other **Jewish Christ-followers** show **overlap** into 2nd century. **Gentile-majority** churches developed **distinct** liturgy, **Christological** teaching, and **canon** consciousness.

Some scholars once cited the **Birkat ha-Minim** (“curse of heretics”) at **Yavneh** as a **formal expulsion** of Christians from synagogues; today that reading is **contested** (evidence for **minim** referring to **multiple** groups; dating and text history uncertain). **Separation** likely proceeded through **local** conflicts—**Christological** claims, **Torah** practice, **Roman** pressure—rather than one **global** decree.

After **70**, **Pharisaic–rabbinic** streams that would eventually produce the **Mishnah** grew in **new** centers; **Christian** communities interpreted **70** as **judgment** on temple rejection of Jesus (Mark 13; Matthew 24)—**theological** rivalry **postdates** shared origins. **Mutual** excommunication was **not** symmetrical in every city: in **Antioch**, **Alexandria**, and **Rome**, social and **literary** boundaries hardened at different speeds (Lieu, *Neither Jew nor Christian?*).

### Diversity and “orthodoxy”

Before **Irenaeus** and **Justin**, labels **“orthodox”** vs. **“heretic”** were **emergent**. **Marcion** (2nd c.) rejected Jewish scripture; **Ignatius** urged unity against **Judaizing**—evidence of **boundary fights**, not settled identity.

## I. The Roman world and the first Christians

### Urban networks

**Meeks**, *The First Urban Christians*: early communities often met in **households**, relied on **patronage**, spanned **social strata** (few elites, many artisans). Women such as **Lydia** (Acts 16), **Phoebe** (Romans 16:1–2), and **Priscilla** (Acts 18; Romans 16:3) appear as **patrons, teachers, and co-workers** in Pauline networks—**widely accepted interpretation** that women held **significant roles**, though office titles and limits are **contested** in light of passages like 1 Corinthians 14:34–35 (textual integrity **contested**).

**Baptism** and **communal meals** (Lord’s Supper) marked belonging; **ethical** codes distinguished members from surrounding **polytheistic** civic life.

### Empire, suspicion, and witnesses

**Well established:** Christians refused **traditional cult** to gods and emperor in ways Romans could view as **atheism** and **disloyalty**. **Pliny** (*Ep.* 10.96–97, ca. 112 CE) asks Trajan how to treat denounced Christians—executions for stubborn refusal, **not** empire-wide policy yet systematized.

**Tacitus** links Christians to Nero’s fire (64 CE) with **hostile** tone. **Persecution** was **local and episodic** before later imperial campaigns—not uniform martyrdom myth (**widely accepted interpretation**).

### Scripture and authority

By early **2nd century**, **Paul’s letters** and **Gospels** circulated; **Didache** gives **community instruction**; **1 Clement** (Rome to Corinth) appeals to **apostolic order**. **Canon** was **not closed**; **Marcion’s** narrow canon provoked **counter-lists** (**contested** timeline of fixation).

**Ignatius of Antioch** (ca. 110 CE, on traditional dating) urges **bishop** unity and **distinct** from Judaism—signpost of **institutional** Christianity forming.

### What did not happen yet

To respect the chapter’s stopping point: **Constantine**, **Nicaea** (325 CE), **Augustine**, medieval **Crusades**, and **medieval rabbinic** maturity lie **outside** this narrative. First-century and early second-century Christians had **no** New Testament table of contents, **no** universal papacy, and **no** single language of faith beyond emerging patterns. Islam lies **centuries** in the future. Later comparative work must not **backfill** those futures into this period.

### Ending point

Christianity in the **early second century** remained **plural**: Greek and Syriac-speaking communities, **Jewish** and **gentile** members in some regions, **competing** Christologies, **no** Nicaea, **no** imperial church. The **sequence**—Jesus’ activity, crucifixion, resurrection belief, Jerusalem community, Paul’s gentile mission, Gospel writing, 70 CE, gradual **border-making**—is the historical story. It was **not** one event and **not** predetermined.

## The Git metaphor (limited)

- Jesus-followers **cloned** Jewish scripture and festival memory, then **patched** interpretation around Jesus.  
- **Branches:** Pauline gentile churches vs. Jerusalem Torah-observant followers—**merge conflicts** over circumcision **not** resolved in one commit.  
- **Gospels** are **parallel forks** of oral tradition with **different diffs**, not one synchronized repo.  
- **Judaism** did not sit static on `main` while Christianity **forked** at a single SHA; both **continued developing**, and **rabbinic Judaism** is largely **post-70** growth on a **different branch** of Jewish history.
