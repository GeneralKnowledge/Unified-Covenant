# Chapter 4: The Emergence of Islam

*From seventh-century Arabia to an early Islamic polity at the end of the first century AH*

---

## How to read this chapter

[Chapter 3](./03-the-emergence-of-christianity.md) ended in the early second century CE with Jewish and Christian communities still overlapping in identity, practice, and debate. Islam did not appear in a cultural vacuum. It emerged in Late Antique Arabia and the exhausted borderlands of the Byzantine and Sasanian empires, associated with Muhammad and the Qur’an, and rapidly became the foundation of a Muslim community (*umma*) and caliphal state.

This chapter is historical reconstruction, not theological proof or disproof. [THE_UNIFIED_COVENANT.md](../THE_UNIFIED_COVENANT.md) remains a separate speculative essay and is not project doctrine.

Evidence labels match earlier chapters: **Well established**, **Widely accepted interpretation**, **Contested**, **Religious tradition**, **Unknown / speculative**.

**Central question:** How did a religious movement associated with Muhammad in seventh-century Arabia become the foundation of a rapidly expanding Muslim community and polity—and what can historians responsibly reconstruct about that process?

The chapter resists two simplifications: treating later Islamic tradition as a verbatim seventh-century transcript, and treating late compilation of sources as reason to dismiss all traditional reports without individual assessment.

## A. Arabia and the Late Antique world before Islam

### Political geography and the Hijaz

Western and central Arabia in the sixth and early seventh centuries were not a uniform “desert” cut off from empires. Tribal confederations (*qaba’il*), kinship, patronage (*wala’*), and customary law structured social life alongside poetry, oral prestige, and negotiated alliances. Political authority was often local and contingent; large-scale unity usually required external subsidy, religious prestige, or military success.

Mecca and Yathrib (later Medina) in the Hijaz are central to Islamic memory. **Religious tradition** presents Mecca as a sanctuary city with the Ka‘ba and pilgrimage (*hajj*). Historians ask how large, how economically important, and how connected Mecca was to long-distance trade.

**Contested:** Patricia Crone argued in *Meccan Trade and the Rise of Islam* (1987) that exaggerated spice-route narratives and Meccan commercial dominance do not match what can be documented for the Hijaz; revisionists and defenders of modified trade models continue to debate routes, goods, and Mecca’s regional role (Asad Q. Ahmed, *The Religious Economy of the Hijaz*; recent syntheses in *The Cambridge History of Islam* vol. 1).

**Widely accepted interpretation:** Arabia participated in Late Antique networks—pastoralism, local agriculture, regional trade, raiding, and tribute—while empires competed for clients on the margins. **Well established:** the Byzantine–Sasanian rivalry shaped the northern and eastern Arabian fringes through client kings, conflicts, and religious patronage.

### Empires, clients, and the sixth-century crisis

**Well established:** the Ghassanids (Byzantine-aligned, largely Arab Christian) and Lakhmids (Sasanian-aligned) acted as buffer polities in Syria and Iraq. Himyar in south Arabia had a complex history including Jewish royal influence and Christian missions before Aksumite and Sasanian interventions (Bowersock, *The Throne of Adulis*; Robin, works on pre-Islamic Arabia).

The early seventh-century Byzantine–Sasanian war (602–628 CE) devastated Syria, Mesopotamia, and Egypt, disrupted taxation and agriculture, and left both empires financially and militarily strained when Arab-led armies expanded in the 630s–640s (**widely accepted interpretation**; Kaegi, *Byzantium and the Early Islamic Conquests*).

### Religious diversity

Arabia and its borderlands hosted polytheistic cults, Christian communities (Miaphysite, Nestorian, and other alignments), Jewish settlements and royal Jewish traditions in the south, and monotheistic currents sometimes grouped under ḥanīf in later Muslim literature (**contested** as a pre-Islamic category—see Uri Rubin on *ḥanīf*).

**Well established** for regions outside the Hijaz: Christianity and Judaism left inscriptional, literary, and archaeological traces (Najran martyrology traditions; Jewish communities in Yathrib reported in later sira—see Section E). For the Hijaz specifically, direct contemporary evidence for religious life before Muhammad is thinner; much depends on Qur’an, later sira, and comparative inference.

**Do not assume** pre-Islamic Arabia was uniformly “idolatrous” on the model of later Muslim polemical lists alone. **Do not assume** every Arabian community shared one religion.

Poetry (*shi‘r*) functioned as a public archive of honor, genealogy, and moral vocabulary. Pre-Islamic odes ( *mu‘allaqāt* and related corpora, transmitted and edited later) show Arab elites articulating courage, loyalty, fate, and sometimes monotheistic language alongside polytheistic cult references. Historians treat this poetry as **contested** evidence for “pre-Islamic religion” because transmission is late and literary, yet it confirms that oral performance shaped how Arabs named the sacred, the tribe, and the hero long before the Qur’an’s codification.

Yathrib (Medina) appears in sources as an oasis settlement with date cultivation and tribal quarters— Aws, Khazraj, and Jewish clans such as Banu Nadir and Banu Qurayẓa in later narratives. Archaeology of the Hijaz cities remains limited compared with the Levant; population estimates and urban layout in the early seventh century are unknown / speculative beyond broad inference. What is **widely accepted interpretation** is that Medina’s internal feuds (the *Battle of Bu‘āth* in tradition) created a context in which an external arbitrator with a unifying message could be welcomed—whether that matches every detail of sira is **contested**.

Christian–Jewish conflict on the Arabian fringe appears in the Najran martyrology (sixth century), preserved in Syriac and Greek traditions and echoed in Qur’an 3:55–61 and 85:4–8 (**widely accepted interpretation** that religious pluralism and persecution were known before Islam; exact historical core of Najran events **contested**). Such episodes show Arabia’s connection to imperial theology (Byzantine Miaphysite vs. Jewish polity in south Arabia) rather than an isolated pagan monolith.

The Quraysh of Mecca appear in Islamic memory as custodians of the Ka‘ba sanctuary and as a commercial elite. Crone’s challenge pushed scholars to distinguish demonstrated long-distance trade in incense, leather, and regional goods from legendary Meccan control of spice routes to Syria. Modified positions allow Mecca as a regional religious and trading node without making it a “Venice of the desert.” Either way, Muhammad’s movement arose in a society where sanctuary prestige, kinship status, and customary obligations (*muruwwa* values) mattered as much as formal state law—which did not exist in the Hijaz in imperial form.

### Questions this section leaves open

| Question | Status |
|----------|--------|
| Size and economy of Mecca/Medina in the 6th–7th c. | **Contested** / limited archaeology |
| Jewish/Christian presence in the Hijaz | **Widely accepted interpretation** for Medina; **contested** details |
| Mecca as long-distance trade hub | **Contested** (Crone vs. modified trade models) |

## B. Sources for Muhammad and early Islam

### The source problem

Historians must separate (1) the date of an event, (2) the date of a surviving text, and (3) the genre and purpose of that text. For early Islam, no continuous contemporary biography of Muhammad survives. Reconstruction combines the Qur’an, later narrative compilations, documentary and material evidence, and non-Muslim reports—each with distinct limits.

### The Qur’an

The Qur’an is the earliest extensive Arabic corpus linked to the movement (**well established** as a seventh-century textual tradition; precise redaction history **contested**). It is not a biography: it presupposes disputes, audiences, and events but rarely narrates Muhammad’s life continuously. It is essential for doctrine, language, chronology of preaching themes (in debated order), and engagement with Jews, Christians, and Arab society.

**Religious tradition:** the Qur’an is uncreated divine speech revealed to Muhammad. Historical study investigates composition, recitation, writing, and standardization without adjudicating supernatural causation (Section D).

### Sira, hadith, and early historiography

Sira (prophetic biography) in the form known to modern readers depends on Ibn Ishaq (d. ca. 767 CE), transmitted especially through Ibn Hisham (d. 834 CE) and cited by al-Tabari (d. 923 CE). These works preserve earlier material but are compiled centuries after the events (**well established** chronology of compilation).

**Widely accepted interpretation:** sira reflects developing communal memory, legal and theological needs, and political legitimation—not pure fiction, not court transcript. Historians triangulate: episodes attested in Qur’an, multiple isnads, early poetry, or external sources weigh differently from isolated late reports.

Hadith (reports of Muhammad’s words and deeds) were massively collected in the 8th–9th centuries (Bukhari, Muslim, etc.). For 7th-century events, hadith are secondary unless supported elsewhere; for emerging norms of the 8th century onward, they are primary evidence of how Muslims remembered Muhammad.

Al-Tabari’s *History* synthesizes earlier traditions with explicit chains; modern scholars use it as a witness to tradition, not automatic fact.

### Material and documentary evidence

**Well established:** Arabic epigraphy, papyri, and coins from the 7th century document administrative, fiscal, and religious change (Grohmann, *Arabic Papyri*; Hoyland, *Seeing Islam as Others Saw It*; Foss, *Arab–Byzantine Coins*).

Early Qur’an manuscripts (Section D) witness textual traditions; radiocarbon dates on parchment date animal death, not necessarily inking or literary composition.

Non-Muslim sources: Syriac, Greek, Armenian, and Coptic texts mention Arab conquerors, Muhammad, and doctrine—often brief, hostile or puzzled, and chronologically uneven. The Armenian history attributed to Sebeos (traditionally ca. 661 CE) includes an early narrative of Muhammad and community formation; authorship and date remain debated (Hoyland; Gero). Doctrina Jacobi (640 CE) mentions a prophet among Saracens—**contested** identification with Muhammad.

### Constitution of Medina

A document known as the Constitution of Medina (*ṣaḥīfat al-Madīna*) appears in sira and is treated by many scholars as preserving an early treaty or treaty cluster between Muhammad and Medinan factions (Wellhausen; Serjeant; **contested** unitary vs. multiple agreements—R. B. Serjeant; Michael Lecker). Authentication debates concern wording, date, and extent, not necessarily total fabrication.

### What can be reconstructed?

| Aspect | Evidence profile |
|--------|------------------|
| Muhammad as preacher and leader in Hijaz | **Widely accepted interpretation** (Qur’an + tradition + later empire) |
| Exact birth year, many biographical anecdotes | **Contested** / tradition-heavy |
| Chronology of major battles and treaties | **Traditional chronology** widely used; **contested** in detail |
| Supernatural revelation | **Religious tradition**; outside historical verification |

Fred Donner’s *Muhammad and the Believers* (2010) argues that the earliest movement may have been a broad monotheistic coalition (“Believers”) including Jews and Christians under a shared ethical monotheism, with a sharper “Muslim” confessional identity emerging later under the Umayyads. Critics respond that the Qur’an already marks distinctive ritual and communal boundaries; supporters reply that practice on the ground may have lagged text. The thesis is **contested** but useful: it warns against reading fully developed fiqh and creed back into the 620s.

For hadith specifically, Joseph Schacht’s mid-twentieth-century legal analysis argued that many attributed Prophetic rulings grew from later regional practice retrojected to Muhammad—a **contested** generalization, but a permanent reminder that isnād lists authenticate transmission, not automatically fact. Modern Muslim scholarship and Western philology both pursue matn (text) analysis and cross-reference with Qur’an and archaeology.

Non-Muslim Syriac chronicles from the seventh century (e.g. Thomas the Presbyter, 640 CE entry on Arab victory; Sebeos on Muhammad’s legislation and Abrahamic lineage) provide roughly contemporary external perspective but not independent biography. They confirm that outsiders soon linked Arab success to a prophetic figure and monotheist preaching, while misdating or misunderstanding details (**widely accepted interpretation** in Hoyland’s survey).

## C. Muhammad’s life and preaching

**Note:** Dates below follow Islamic traditional chronology unless marked otherwise. **Religious tradition** holds Muhammad was born in Mecca ca. 570 CE (Year of the Elephant in tradition), of the Hashim clan of Quraysh. Historical certainty on birth date and many childhood stories is low.

### Meccan period

**Widely accepted interpretation:** Muhammad’s marriage to Khadija placed him within Meccan commercial and kin networks (details from sira). Traditional accounts describe revelation beginning ca. 610 CE in Ramadan at Mount Hira—**religious tradition**; historians treat this as the formative moment of the movement’s self-understanding, not as an independently datable event.

**Well established** from the Qur’an’s content (Meccan surahs generally): preaching tawḥīd (God’s oneness), judgment, resurrection, moral accountability, care for the poor, and critique of polytheism and social injustice. The Qur’an retells and reinterprets Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and other figures—engaging Jewish and Christian narratives without matching them point-for-point (Section D).

Opposition in Mecca: tradition reports persecution of followers, boycott, and pressure on Muhammad. Causes—theological, economic, tribal honor—are **contested**; Qur’anic polemic against wealthy elites suggests social as well as cultic conflict (**widely accepted interpretation**).

Meccan surahs emphasize yaum al-dīn (Day of Judgment), resurrection, weighing of deeds, and warnings to those who deny accountability. This eschatological urgency situates the movement in a Late Antique world where Jews, Christians, and other Arabs already debated afterlife, divine justice, and moral order. The Qur’an’s insistence that God is one and rejects partners (*shirk*) targets cultic practices associated with Arabian polytheism in tradition and with trinitarian Christology in polemical passages—without assuming every opponent held identical theology.

Early converts in sira—including Abu Bakr, ‘Ali, Zayd b. Haritha, and Khadija in tradition—illustrate kin-based recruitment and manumission stories whose historical cores vary in confidence. Bilal, ‘Ammar, and Sumayya embody persecution memory; their martyrdom narratives are **religious tradition** with **widely accepted interpretation** that some followers faced violence, even if each vignette is not independently verified.

### Hijra and Medinan leadership

Traditional date: Hijra (migration) to Yathrib/Medina in 622 CE—anchor of the Islamic calendar (**religious tradition** and historiographical convention; independent confirmation of exact year limited).

In Medina, Muhammad became arbitrator, leader, and military commander for a mixed community of Emigrants (*Muhajirun*), Helpers (*Ansar*), and tribal clients. **Well established** as historical pattern: religion, kinship, and politics were interwoven—not a modern separation of “church” and “state.”

Warfare (*ghazw*): Badr (trad. 624), Uhud (625), Khandaq/Trench (627) appear in sira and Qur’an (e.g. surahs 3, 8, 33). Outcomes and numbers are tradition-dependent; historical fact of armed conflict between Muhammad’s coalition and Meccan and allied forces is **widely accepted interpretation**.

Treaty of Hudaybiyya (trad. 628) and conquest of Mecca (trad. 630) mark diplomatic and military turning points in sira. Ka‘ba and pilgrimage were incorporated into Muslim ritual geography (**religious tradition**; Qur’an 2:124–127, 3:96–97).

### Final years and death

Muhammad died in Medina in 632 CE (trad. 11 AH)—traditional date well established in Islamic chronology; medical details unknown. By then, much of Arabia had accepted his leadership in varying degrees (**widely accepted interpretation**; Ridda wars after death show incomplete uniformity).

**Critical distinction:** Islam teaches Muhammad is God’s final messenger. Historians can study proclamation, reception, community formation, and consequences; they cannot verify revelation by historical method alone.

**Do not reduce** Muhammad to only mystic or only warlord; evidence supports preacher, judge, diplomat, and commander in seventh-century conditions.

The Farewell Pilgrimage (trad. 632) and final sermon traditions consolidate ritual and ethical teaching—**religious tradition** cited in later law and ethics. Historians use them cautiously as evidence for emerging communal norms, not as stenographic records. Muhammad’s marriages and political alliances (including unions tied to tribal reconciliation in sira) show how kinship politics operated alongside religious leadership; modern moral evaluation differs from historical description, which must report sources without anachronistic simplification.

## D. The Qur’an: revelation, text, and historical context

### Doctrine, history, and interpretation

Three layers must stay distinct:

1. Islamic doctrine: the Qur’an is God’s word, inimitable, preserved.  
2. Historical study: how Arabic recitations were memorized, written, collected, and standardized.  
3. Literary-theological analysis: themes, structure, engagement with Late Antique religion.

### Form and content

The Qur’an comprises 114 surahs of uneven length, generally not chronological in the musḥaf order. Meccan vs. Medinan classification (by place of revelation in tradition) helps thematic chronology; absolute dating of every surah is **contested** (Neuwirth, *The Qur’an and Late Antiquity*; Sinai, *The Qur’an*).

Oral performance (*qirā’a*) was central; written materials ( ṣuḥuf, private codices) existed early (**widely accepted interpretation**; tradition on Zayd b. Thabit and official collection under Abu Bakr and ‘Uthmān—**religious tradition** with historical debate on process).

### Collection and the ‘Uthmanic text

Traditional account: ‘Uthmān (caliph, r. 644–656) ordered a standard consonantal text and disputed codices were suppressed—**religious tradition**; extent of suppression and variant readings **contested**. **Well established:** reading traditions (*qirā’āt*) preserve authorized variant recitations within Islamic norms—not arbitrary corruption.

### Manuscript evidence

Birmingham Mingana 1572a (Cadbury Research Library): radiocarbon on parchment ca. 568–645 CE (95.4% probability, Oxford 2014)—dates animal death, not ink or composition (University of Birmingham FAQ). Script is Hijazi; text aligns with standard surahs (18, 19, 20 portions).

Sana‘a palimpsest (Yemen): lower text shows variant readings vs. standard text—**well established** as evidence for early textual diversity and subsequent standardization (Sadeghi & Bergmann, *Der Islam* 2010; **contested** implications for canonicity).

Dome of the Rock inscriptions (691–692 CE / 72 AH): Qur’anic phrases with occasional variants—material witness to 7th-century text traditions (Grabar, *The Dome of the Rock*).

### Engagement with earlier traditions

The Qur’an presumes Torah, Gospel, and prophetic history while accusing some People of the Book of distortion or error (e.g. 2:75–79, 4:157—interpretation **contested** between Muslim, Jewish, and Christian readers). Parallels with biblical and post-biblical material may reflect shared lore, oral circulation, polemic, or literary dependence—each case evaluated separately (**contested** overall models: traditionist vs. revisionist chronologies in Wansbrough, *Quranic Studies*, vs. mainstream 7th-century dating).

**Do not** treat parallels as proof of copying or correctness without specific analysis.

Angelika Neuwirth and Nicolai Sinai, among others, read the Qur’an as participating in Late Antique homiletic and liturgical discourse—addressing audiences who already know biblical figures but need Arabic proclamation. Surahs often deploy oaths, rhyme, and  repetition suited to oral delivery; meaning unfolds through intertextual cues rather than linear narrative. Chronological ordering schemes (Nöldeke’s classic Meccan/Medinan phases) remain **widely accepted interpretation** as a heuristic, even where individual surah dates are **contested**.

The Birmingham and Sana‘a witnesses do not “prove” or “disprove” the traditional account of ‘Uthmān’s recension; they show that written Qur’anic material circulated within decades of Muhammad’s lifetime and that standardization likely responded to real textual variation, not imaginary later invention alone. Islamic doctrine holds the Uthmanic consonantal skeleton (*rasm*) as preserved; historical study notes diacritical and vocalization systems developed later while the consonantal text stabilized early (**widely accepted interpretation** among philologists, details **contested**).

Qur’anic covenant language (*‘ahd*, *mīthāq*) reworks memories of Moses and Israel (e.g. surah 2’s long Medinan passages) to address the Muslim umma and its neighbors. That is theological reinterpretation in Arabic idiom; historians document the rhetoric and its effects, not adjudicate whether it correctly represents prior scriptures.

## E. The early Muslim community and the Medina period

### Emigrants, Helpers, and tribal bonds

The umma in Medina combined kinship with new obligations—brotherhood between Muhajirun and Ansar, shared ritual (prayer, fasting, alms), and loyalty to Muhammad’s authority. Tribal identities persisted beneath religious affiliation (**widely accepted interpretation**).

### Constitution of Medina

The Constitution tradition lists Jewish clans as parties to security and mutual defense arrangements with Muslims, while Muhammad acts as arbiter (text in Ibn Ishaq/Ibn Hisham). Scholars debate single document vs. layers (**contested**). Historical value: likely early treaty practice; exact clauses uncertain.

### Relations with Jewish groups in Medina

Sources report specific conflicts with particular clans (e.g. Banu Qaynuqa‘, Banu Nadir, Banu Qurayẓa) in different circumstances—siege, expulsion, execution narratives (sira, Tabari). Historians dispute chronology, motives, and scale; modern polemic often flattens these into timeless judgments—explicitly rejected here.

**Well established** as historical pattern: political alliances and breaks occurred along tribal and confessional lines; not all Jews of Medina shared one political fate. **Contested:** Qurayẓa incident details and ethical historiography (Watt, *Muhammad at Medina*; modern reevaluations).

### Ritual, alms, and sacred geography

Prayer direction (qibla) shifted from Jerusalem to Mecca in tradition (Qur’an 2:142–144—**religious tradition** on timing). Zakat/ṣadaqa collection and distribution appear as communal duties. Hajj and ‘umra linked Muslims to Abrahamic memory in Qur’anic rhetoric (2:127, 22:26–27).

**Avoid** reading mature fiqh back into 632 CE.

Medinan surahs regulate war booty, prisoners, hypocrites (*munāfiqūn*), and relations with People of the Book—reflecting ongoing negotiation, not a finished legal code. The change of qibla and incorporation of Meccan pilgrimage into Muslim ritual linked Arabian sacred geography to Abrahamic memory in Qur’anic terms (2:124–127), distinct from Second Temple Jewish temple centrality or Christian Eucharistic focus in Chapter 3.

Git metaphor (limited): later Muslim memory of Medina functions somewhat like a canonical initial commit for communal law—authority and precedent were ascribed to Muhammad’s decisions there—but branches of interpretation (Sunni, Shi‘i, Kharijite) disagreed about which precedents bound the caliph and which required infallible leadership from Ahl al-Bayt. The metaphor illustrates contested continuity, not software genealogy.

## F. The death of Muhammad and the succession crisis

### Death and immediate aftermath

Muhammad’s death (632 CE) ended unique prophetic leadership in **Religious tradition** (Muhammad as final messenger). Historians observe that the Qur’an does not prescribe a single, universally accepted procedure for choosing a political successor—**Widely accepted interpretation** of a source gap—while traditions preserve competing claims, arguments, and expectations at Saqifa and afterward. Sunni and Shi‘i succession theologies developed later; they should not be read fully formed into 632 CE.

### Saqifa and Abu Bakr

Tradition: Ansar gathered at Saqifa Banu Sa‘ida to discuss leadership; Abu Bakr, ‘Umar, and Abu ‘Ubayda intervened; Abu Bakr was acclaimed caliph (khalīfat rasūl Allāh—“successor of God’s messenger”). Competing claims: ‘Ali and Muhammad’s family ( Banu Hashim ) had strong partisan support in later Shi‘i memory.

Historical reconstruction: elite negotiation and precedence arguments (Abu Bakr as elder Companion) within hours of death—details and speeches tradition-dependent. **Do not** project mature Sunni–Shi‘i theology onto 632.

### Ridda wars

Under Abu Bakr, military campaigns subdued Arabian groups who withheld allegiance or followed other leaders (Musaylima, Sajah, etc.). Traditional label “wars of apostasy” may overstate purely religious apostasy; tax, tribal, and political factors widely accepted as co-present (Donner, *Muhammad and the Believers*).

### Emergence of the caliphate

The caliphate became the central political institution of Muslim empire—successor to prophetic authority in governance, not prophethood (**religious tradition**). Early limits of caliphal power vs. tribal autonomy **contested**.

## G. The early caliphate and expansion

### The “Rightly Guided” caliphs (overview)

| Caliph | Traditional reign | Notes (historical sketch) |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| Abu Bakr | 632–634 | Ridda; Syrian raid preparation |
| ‘Umar | 634–644 | Major conquests; administrative patterns |
| ‘Uthmān | 644–656 | Expansion; Qur’anic standardization tradition; assassination |
| ‘Ali | 656–661 | First Fitna; Kufa base |

‘Umar (r. 634–644) is credited in tradition with diwan (register) organization for tribal stipends, calendar reform tied to Hijra, and conquest of Syria and Egypt. **Well established:** expansion accelerated under his reign; specific institutional firsts are tradition-dependent but **widely accepted interpretation** that central fiscal oversight increased. His assassination (644) by a Persian slave in Medina illustrates ongoing internal violence even at the height of success.

### Conquests

**Well established:** Arab–Muslim armies seized Byzantine Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and Sasanian Iraq and Iran within roughly two decades; the Sasanian empire collapsed.

The Battle of Yarmuk (636, traditional date) and fall of Ctesiphon / Qadisiyya complex (637 region) symbolize Byzantine and Sasanian defeat in Muslim historiography; exact tactics and numbers are tradition-dependent. Amr b. al-‘As’s Egypt campaign (from 639) linked North Africa to the new polity. **Well established** from multiple lines: garrison towns (*amsār*—Kufa, Basra, Fustat) anchored Arab tribal settlement while local populations remained largely non-Muslim for generations.

Conquerors often negotiated (*dhimma*-like arrangements in later legal language; early terms varied). Jizya appears in Qur’an 9:29 as a tax associated with People of the Book; implementation in the 640s–680s is **contested** in detail but **widely accepted interpretation** that fiscal differentiation existed. Conversion to Islam could alter tax status; economic incentive thus interacted with religious motivation—neither factor alone explains every region (Morony on Syria; Kennedy on conquests).

**Contested** relative weight of factors: imperial exhaustion, local grievances, tax regimes, Arab tribal military cohesion, religious motivation, plunder, elite defection. No single-cause theory adequate (Kaegi; Hoyland; Kennedy, *The Great Arab Conquests*).

**Well established:** conquest ≠ immediate mass conversion. Arabic gradually spread as administrative language; Christian and Jewish communities persisted for centuries.

### Administration, coinage, architecture

Papyri from Egypt show fiscal transition (**well established**). Coinage evolved from Byzantine/Sasanian imitations to distinctive Islamic types under ‘Abd al-Malik ( 697 reform—widely accepted date). Dome of the Rock, Jerusalem, 72 AH / ca. 691–692 CE—monumental statement of Islamic presence and Qur’anic theology (inscriptions emphasize tawḥīd, Jesus as servant/messenger, not Son in Trinitarian sense).

### First Fitna and Umayyad consolidation

‘Uthmān’s assassination in 656 CE ended the period later Sunnis would call the Rightly Guided Caliphate and opened internal Muslim war. Sources describe grievances over governor appointments, fiscal centralization, and failure to punish attackers—**contested** as primary vs. post hoc explanations. ‘Ali moved his seat toward Iraq (Kufa); ‘A’isha, Talha, and Zubayr opposed him at the Battle of the Camel (656). Mu‘awiya in Damascus demanded justice for ‘Uthmān before accepting ‘Ali’s authority.

Ṣiffīn (657) ended with arbitration (*taḥkīm*) that Kharijites rejected as human judgment over divine command. ‘Ali’s assassination by a Kharijite (661) removed the last caliph widely linked to Medina’s early coalition. Mu‘awiya’s accession established Umayyad rule (661–750), shifting capital toward Syria and treating the caliphate as hereditary kingship in practice—**religious tradition** disputes legitimacy; historical fact of dynastic continuity is **well established**.

The Second Fitna (680s) followed Yazīd and Karbala (680, Ashura memory in Shi‘i tradition) and Ibn al-Zubayr’s Mecca-based challenge. ‘Abd al-Malik (685–705) restored Umayyad control, built the Dome of the Rock, reformed coinage, and promoted Arabic administration. Donner’s “Believers” thesis (**contested**) suggests that under these Umayyads a narrower Muslim confessional identity crystallized from a broader monotheist movement; opponents argue the Qur’an already presupposes a distinct umma.

Chapter endpoint: ca. 700 CE—a recognizable Umayyad caliphate controlling much of the former Byzantine Near East and Sasanian Iran, with garrison centers and frontier zones reaching toward Central Asia; Iberia lay outside Muslim rule until the 711 CE crossing (after this chapter’s close). Rule was neither uniform nor majority-Muslim in every district. Mature legal schools, the Abbasid revolution (749), and classical kalām fall outside this chapter’s scope.

## H. Jewish and Christian communities under early Muslim rule

### Qur’anic discourse

The Qur’an acknowledges earlier scripture and prophets while critiquing some Jewish and Christian claims (4:171, 5:17, 9:30—theological polemic in context). Ahl al-Kitāb (“People of the Book”) is a Qur’anic category; legal dhimma status developed later (**widely accepted interpretation**).

### Practice in conquered territories

**Widely accepted interpretation:** early conquests often preserved local administrators, languages, and religious institutions for decades. Syriac- and Coptic-speaking Christians continued liturgy; Babylonian and Palestinian Jewish communities persisted under new fiscal masters. Papyri from Egypt (PERF, APH collections) show Greek and Coptic continuing in tax records while Arabic gradually entered chancery practice—**well established** as a slow transition, **contested** timeline per district.

Jizya and related poll/tribute categories appear in Qur’an and later juristic manuals; mapping them onto every seventh-century  treaty is **contested**. Some regions experienced hardship and dispossession; others saw elite cooperation. Neither idealized convivencia nor blanket persecution fits the patchwork evidence.

### Diversity

Miaphysite (often labeled Jacobite), Nestorian, Melkite, Jewish, Zoroastrian, and other groups differed by region. Iraq’s Nestorian church and Syria’s Miaphysite majority illustrate that “Christian” experience under Islam was not one story. Patriarchs and exilarchs sometimes negotiated with caliphs—**well established** for later centuries; Seventh-century documentation is thinner but directionally similar.

John of Damascus (eighth century) wrote later but reflects ongoing Christian theological response to Islam in Umayyad Syria—outside this chapter’s core period yet a witness to continuity of debate.

**Do not** import classical fiqh dhimma codes back into 632 as if uniformly applied.

## I. The formation of Islamic identity and authority

### Prophet, Qur’an, and sunna

Finality of Muhammad’s prophethood (khātama al-nabiyyīn, Qur’an 33:40) is central Islamic doctrine. Authority sources crystallized gradually: Qur’an, prophetic example (sunna), consensus, reasoning—classic usūl al-fiqh formulated later (**well established** chronology of legal theory).

### Hadith and scholars

Hadith corpus growth postdates Muhammad; isnād criticism in the eighth–ninth centuries shaped Sunni canonical collections. Shi‘a collections privilege Ahl al-Bayt reports.

### Sectarian trajectories

Kharijite, Shi‘i, and proto-Sunni alignments emerged from Fitna politics and theology over decades—labels anachronistic if read fully formed in 632. **Well established:** ‘Ali’s partisans remembered Saqifa as usurpation; Sunni tradition validated Abu Bakr–‘Umar sequence.

### Ritual

Five prayers, Ramadan fast, hajj, shahāda—Qur’anic and traditional foundations exist; many details stabilized under later scholarship.

## Historical ledger

### Inherited traditions (Late Antiquity → early Islam)

- Monotheism, prophetic figures, scripture, covenant language, prayer, fasting, almsgiving, pilgrimage, resurrection, judgment.  
- Jewish and Christian communities in Arabia and conquered lands—demonstrable historical contact; theological “continuation” claims remain tradition-specific.  
- Byzantine–Sasanian political inheritance (tax, cities, elites).

### Emerging distinctions

- Qur’an as authoritative Arabic revelation corpus.  
- Muhammad as prophet and founding leader (finality = religious doctrine).  
- Umma and caliphate linking faith and polity.  
- Distinctive ritual calendar and Meccan cult reorientation.  
- Arabic imperial culture under Umayyads.

### Unresolved historical questions

1. Chronology and circumstances of specific sira events.  
2. Qur’an composition vs. collection timeline.  
3. Mecca’s economic role.  
4. Constitution of Medina authentic form.  
5. Medina Jewish clan conflicts—motives and facts.  
6. Saqifa—exact proceedings.  
7. Ridda—religious vs. political labels.  
8. Conquest causes weighting.  
9. Timing of Sunni / Shi‘i / Kharijite identity formation.  
10. Extent of 7th-century “Islam” as confession vs. monotheist coalition (Donner debate).
